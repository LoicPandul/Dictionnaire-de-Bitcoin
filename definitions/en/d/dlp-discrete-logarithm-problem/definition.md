Mathematical problem on which the security of public-key cryptographic algorithms, notably those used on Bitcoin, relies. In a cyclic group of order $q$, with a generator $g$, given an equation of the form $g^x = h$, $x$ is called the discrete logarithm of $h$ with respect to the base $g$, modulo $q$. Put simply, the task is to determine the exponent $x$ when $g$, $h$, and $q$ are known. The discrete logarithm is thus the inverse of exponentiation in a finite cyclic group. However, for large values of $q$, solving the discrete logarithm problem is considered computationally hard. This property is exploited to secure many cryptographic protocols, such as the Diffie-Hellman protocol for key exchange. The discrete logarithm is also used in elliptic curve cryptography (ECC), among others in the ECDSA algorithm (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm). In the context of elliptic curves, the discrete logarithm problem extends to finding a scalar $k$ such that $k \cdot G = K$, where $G$ and $K$ are points on the curve, and $\cdot$ represents the point multiplication operation. In the context of Bitcoin, standard transactions use either ECDSA or Schnorr signatures to lock UTXOs. Both rely on the infeasibility of computing the discrete logarithm.
