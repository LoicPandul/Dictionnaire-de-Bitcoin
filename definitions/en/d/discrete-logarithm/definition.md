Mathematical problem used in some public-key cryptographic algorithms. In a cyclic group of order $q$, with a generator $g$, given an equation of the form $g^x = h$, $x$ is called the discrete logarithm of $h$ with respect to the base $g$, modulo $q$. In simple terms, the task is to find the exponent $x$ when $g$, $h$, and $q$ are known. The discrete logarithm is therefore the inverse of exponentiation in a finite cyclic group. However, for large values of $q$, solving the discrete logarithm problem is considered computationally hard. This property is exploited to secure many cryptographic protocols, such as the Diffie-Hellman key exchange.

The discrete logarithm is also used in elliptic curve cryptography (ECC), notably in the ECDSA algorithm (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm). In the context of elliptic curves, the discrete logarithm problem becomes finding a scalar $k$ such that $k \cdot G = K$, where $G$ and $K$ are points on the curve, and $\cdot$ represents point multiplication. In the context of Bitcoin, scripts can use either ECDSA or Schnorr signatures to lock UTXOs. Both rely on the infeasibility of computing the discrete logarithm.
