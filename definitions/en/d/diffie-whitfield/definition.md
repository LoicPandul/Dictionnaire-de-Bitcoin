American cryptographer, coauthor with Martin Hellman of “New Directions in Cryptography” (November 1976), the paper that founded public-key cryptography and described the key exchange that bears both their names. Born on June 5, 1944, in Washington, he earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics at MIT in 1965, worked at the MITRE Corporation and then at the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, before joining Hellman at Stanford, where he was a graduate student and research assistant from 1975 to 1978.

The paper, received by *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* on June 3, 1976, starts from an observation: to encrypt, two correspondents must first share a secret key over a secure channel, such as a courier. Diffie and Hellman propose a key exchange. Alice and Bob each choose a secret number, publish the result of a modular exponentiation computed from that number, then each compute the same shared secret on their own. An observer who sees the two public values go by cannot recover that secret, because doing so would require computing a discrete logarithm. The paper also describes the digital signature, which is produced with a private key and verified with the matching public key. Ralph Merkle was working separately on key distribution over an insecure channel, and the paper cites his work.

Diffie then headed security research at Northern Telecom (1978-1991), and became chief security officer of Sun Microsystems (1991-2009). He received the 2015 Turing Award with Hellman, “for inventing and promulgating both asymmetric public-key cryptography, including its application to digital signatures, and a practical cryptographic key-exchange method.”

Bitcoin uses the elliptic-curve variant of this exchange, ECDH. In the v2 P2P transport of BIP-0324, two nodes derive from it the keys that encrypt their connection. In the Silent Payments of BIP-0352, the sender and the recipient each derive the same one-time address from it, without having to communicate.
