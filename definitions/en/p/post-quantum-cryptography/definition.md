Set of cryptographic algorithms designed to remain secure against a quantum computer as well as a classical computer, while running on today's ordinary computers. They rely on mathematical problems that no one knows how to solve efficiently, even with a quantum computer: finding a preimage of a hash function, finding a short vector in a lattice, decoding a random error-correcting code, or computing an isogeny between elliptic curves. Their role is to replace RSA, Diffie-Hellman key exchange, and elliptic curve cryptography, which includes Bitcoin's ECDSA and Schnorr signatures, all of which Shor's algorithm would break on a large enough machine.

The need comes from an asymmetry between two families of cryptography. Public-key cryptography (RSA, elliptic curves) relies on the factorization of large integers or on the discrete logarithm, two problems that Shor's algorithm solves in a polynomial number of steps on a quantum computer. Symmetric cryptography and hash functions are affected, for key search and preimage search, only by Grover's algorithm, which halves their number of bits of security. According to the draft NIST IR 8547 (November 2024), symmetric primitives with at least 128 bits of classical security remain acceptable, and NIST does not plan to change them. Post-quantum cryptography therefore mostly means the replacements for public-key cryptography, for its two uses: establishing a shared secret key (encryption, key exchange) and signing.

These replacements fall into families, according to the hard problem they rely on. Hash-based schemes, like SLH-DSA, depend only on the security of a hash function; they are the heirs of the Lamport signature. Lattice-based schemes, like ML-KEM, ML-DSA, or FN-DSA, rely on the hardness of problems on lattices of points in a large number of dimensions, such as finding a short vector or solving a system of noisy linear equations. Code-based schemes, like HQC, rely on the hardness of decoding a random linear code, that is, of recovering the errors added to a codeword. Multivariate schemes rely on solving systems of polynomial equations in several variables, and isogeny-based schemes, like SQIsign, on computing links between elliptic curves. These families differ in maturity, in the size of their keys and signatures, and in computation speed.

The youth of some families is a real risk. A post-quantum scheme must resist every attacker, classical computers included: in 2022, Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru broke SIKE, an isogeny-based key exchange scheme then in the fourth round of the NIST competition, with a classical attack that recovers the key of the level 1 version in about ten minutes on a single processor core. The same year, Ward Beullens recovered the secret key of Rainbow, a finalist multivariate signature scheme, in 53 hours of computation on average on a laptop, for the level 1 version. Hybrid solutions limit this risk: they combine a post-quantum algorithm and a proven classical algorithm, and stay secure as long as one of the two holds. NIST accepts them, presenting them as a transitional step that adds complexity.

The urgency is not the same for encryption and for signatures. An attacker can record encrypted exchanges today to decrypt them the day a quantum computer is available: this is the "harvest now, decrypt later" threat, which NIST presents as one of the main reasons to migrate without waiting. NIST formulates it with Mosca's theorem: if data must stay secret for $X$ years and the migration takes $Y$, the migration must begin before $X + Y$ exceeds the time $Z$ until a CRQC appears. An authentication signature, for its part, generally protects only the moment when it is verified. Bitcoin is an exception, because a public key visible on the blockchain keeps protecting funds as long as they do not move: the post-quantum signature entry details this case.

On Bitcoin, post-quantum cryptography concerns mostly signatures, but not only. The encrypted connections between nodes defined by BIP-0324 use an ECDH key exchange on secp256k1, which Shor's algorithm can also break. In May 2026, Olaoluwa Osuntokun started a discussion on the bitcoindev mailing list to replace this exchange with ML-KEM, or to combine it with ML-KEM in a hybrid version, stressing that changing the peer-to-peer protocol does not require the broad agreement that a consensus change demands. For signatures, Bitcoin Optech notes that quantum-resistant alternatives exist, but with much larger keys and signatures, and that most developers seem to prefer waiting until migration becomes necessary. As of October 2026, the Bitcoin Core code implements no post-quantum algorithm.

The field developed after the publication of Shor's algorithm in 1994, and the PQCrypto conference has brought it together since 2006. In late 2016, NIST issued a call for submissions to standardize quantum-resistant public-key algorithms. In July 2022 it selected its first 4 winners, 3 based on lattices and 1 on hash functions, then on August 13, 2024 published the first 3 standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, derived from SPHINCS+). Falcon is to follow in FIPS 206, still in preparation in October 2026, and HQC was selected on March 11, 2025. The same NIST plans in its draft IR 8547 to disallow ECDSA, EdDSA, and RSA after 2035, and to deprecate them after 2030 for parameters with 112 bits of security. The detailed history of this process appears in the NIST post-quantum cryptography standardization entry.

*Also abbreviated PQC, and sometimes called quantum-resistant cryptography. Not to be confused with quantum cryptography, which uses the properties of quantum physics to protect communications and requires dedicated hardware, whereas post-quantum cryptography is a set of ordinary algorithms that defend against quantum computers.*
