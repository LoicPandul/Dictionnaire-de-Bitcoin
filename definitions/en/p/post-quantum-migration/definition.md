Move of bitcoin from outputs whose security relies on ECDSA or Schnorr to outputs that resist a quantum computer. It first requires Bitcoin's consensus to accept new output types, most often through a soft fork, and then holders to move their funds to them. It also raises a question that technology does not settle: what to do with the coins whose owner will never move them.

The timeline of the migration is constrained by the arrival of a CRQC, the Q-day, whose date is unknown. The migration involves three workstreams. The first is technical: creating post-quantum output types. BIP-0360 proposes P2MR, an output close to P2TR without a key path spend, which protects against the long-exposure attack without waiting for a new signature scheme, as long as the public key is not reused. In July 2026, Pieter Wuille proposed on Delving Bitcoin to deploy P2TRv2, a variant of P2TR with a post-quantum signature opcode, and a P2MR-based type for the long term. On the signature side, BIP-0360 cites ML-DSA and SLH-DSA, two NIST standards, as algorithms to examine, without choosing one.

The second workstream concerns users. Let's take an example. Alice holds 1 BTC at a P2WPKH address. She must install a wallet that supports the new output type, create a post-quantum address, then send her funds there in an ordinary transaction, signed one last time with ECDSA. BIP-0361 points out that coordinating wallets, exchanges, miners, and custodians has historically taken years, and makes this an argument for setting a public deadline.

The third workstream concerns coins that will not migrate: dormant funds, lost keys, owners who will never have known. Estimates of the share of bitcoin held in outputs with an exposed public key range from about a quarter to about half of the supply (from 4 to almost 10 million BTC) according to the Chaincode Labs report of May 2025, and BIP-0361 gives over 34% as of March 1, 2026. Chaincode distinguishes two options. Burning these funds, that is, making them unusable through a consensus change, treats the quantum vulnerability as a protocol bug. For Jameson Lopp, whose arguments were relayed by Optech on April 4, 2025, most holders would rather see their funds destroyed than stolen by the owner of a quantum computer, and a deadline announced in advance pushes people to migrate in time. Opponents consider that such destruction would be confiscation, and that not freezing users' funds is one of Bitcoin's inviolable properties. The opposite option, doing nothing, leaves these funds to the first people who get a CRQC. BIP-0361 in fact lists three families of proposals: letting anyone steal these coins, allowing limited theft, which in its view would lead to transaction replacement battles in which miners supplement their revenue with recovered coins, or allowing no one to.

BIP-0361, proposed by Jameson Lopp and five coauthors, a draft assigned on February 11, 2026, sets a two-phase timeline. Phase A forbids sending funds to vulnerable outputs 160,000 blocks, about 3 years, after activation. Phase B, 2 years later, subjects ECDSA and Schnorr spends to a rescue protocol, not yet defined: the holder would prove knowledge that a quantum attacker is very unlikely to have, for example that of a parent key of a hardened BIP-0032 derivation, with a ZK-STARK proof or a commit/reveal protocol, two research avenues cited by the BIP. These protocols do not a priori cover P2PK outputs, where no such asymmetry is known. The version published on bitcoindev on July 12, 2025 instead made ECDSA and Schnorr spends invalid about five years after activation, with any recovery by ZK proof deferred to a separate BIP.

These proposals are drafts, with no activation decided. They are not the only ones: Conduition sketched DropKick in August 2026, a commit/reveal rescue protocol for those who have not migrated before Q-day, and the 2026 discussions on Delving Bitcoin and bitcoindev compare the candidate output types. NIST, for its part, sets deadlines for its own standards, with a ban on ECDSA after 2035 in the November 2024 draft of its report IR 8547; these dates do not apply to Bitcoin.
