Digital signature scheme designed to remain secure against a quantum computer, and therefore against Shor's algorithm, which breaks ECDSA and Schnorr, the two signatures of Bitcoin. Like any signature scheme, it consists of three operations: generating a key pair, signing a message with the private key, and verifying the signature with the public key. It differs from ECDSA and Schnorr in the hard problem it relies on, a problem that even a CRQC cannot solve efficiently, and in a much higher cost in bytes. As of October 2026, no post-quantum signature is activated on Bitcoin.

Candidate schemes are grouped by the family of post-quantum cryptography they derive from. In the hash-based family, security depends only on a hash function, as in the Lamport signature: SLH-DSA, derived from SPHINCS+ and standardized by NIST in FIPS 205, and XMSS, a stateful scheme whose private key changes with every signature. In the lattice-based family, ML-DSA, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium and standardized in FIPS 204, and FN-DSA, derived from Falcon, whose FIPS 206 standard remains in preparation as of October 2026. In the isogeny-based family, SQIsign, more recent and less battle-tested. NIST published FIPS 204 and FIPS 205 on August 13, 2024. According to FIPS 204, ML-DSA is considered secure, even against an adversary with a large quantum computer.

The price shows in the size of keys and signatures. The report published by Chaincode in May 2025 compares the schemes with Schnorr, whose public key is 32 bytes and signature 64 bytes. Falcon-512 requires a 897-byte key and a 666-byte signature, a signature about 10 times larger. CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA-44) requires a 1,312-byte key and a 2,420-byte signature, about 38 times larger. SPHINCS+ 128s keeps a 32-byte key but signs with 7,856 bytes, more than 100 times Schnorr. A Lamport signature reaches 8,192 bytes for a 16,384-byte key, and an XMSS signature about 2,500 bytes with a tree of height 10 (the table says 2,440, without specifying the parameters). SQIsign signed with 177 bytes in its first specification (2023), about 2.8 times Schnorr, and with 200 bytes, about 3.1 times, in version 3.0 of September 2026. The report estimated the 2023 version at about 135,000 times slower than ECDSA to sign and about 830 times slower to verify; version 3.0 signs about 51 times faster and verifies about 8 times faster than the 2023 one. Hash-based schemes like SPHINCS+ thus offer very compact keys and large signatures, with Lamport as an exception because of its 16,384-byte key; lattice-based schemes offer a more balanced compromise; and isogeny-based schemes offer small signatures but very slow computation.

These bytes weigh on Bitcoin because block space is limited: larger signatures take up more weight in each transaction, so they cost more in fees and reduce the number of transactions per block. For hash-based signatures, Optech reported in October 2026 that a reduction in witness weight strong enough to make them competitive would make the storage needed to archive the chain grow to the order of a terabyte per year, according to Conduition's estimate. Wallets raise a second problem: Kudinov and Nick, cited by Optech in January 2026, found no alternative applicable to BIP-0032, MuSig2 (BIP-0327), or FROST for hash-based signatures, although other work cited by Optech in July 2025 indicates that equivalents exist.

The need to migrate comes from a peculiarity of Bitcoin. A signature generally protects only the moment of its verification: NIST writes in draft IR 8547 (November 2024) that an authentication system remains secure as long as the algorithms and keys are secure at the time of authentication. On Bitcoin, a public key revealed on the blockchain keeps protecting the funds as long as they have not moved, and an attacker with a CRQC could derive the private key from it. The entries on long exposure attacks and short exposure attacks detail the two situations; BIP-0360 notes that protection against short exposure might require post-quantum signatures.

None of these signatures is in Bitcoin yet. The Bitcoin Core code of October 7, 2026 contains no implementation of them in its `src/` directory, and BIP-0360, which defines P2MR, introduces none: it cites ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as examples and envisions several signatures for redundancy. Its earlier versions went further. The first variant, named P2QRH, initially targeted Falcon, with SQIsign and other algorithms to follow; version 0.3.0 of October 21, 2024 replaced XMSS with Dilithium, version 0.6.0 of January 20, 2025 removed SQIsign for performance reasons, the March 2025 versions proposed FN-DSA-512, ML-DSA-44, and SLH-DSA-SHAKE-128s, and version 0.8.0 of July 7, 2025 reduced the number of algorithms from 3 to 2. According to the Chaincode report, Ethan Heilman argues for supporting two signatures, Falcon for its size and performance, and SPHINCS+ as a more reliable but less efficient fallback, while Jonas Nick recalls that any scheme added to consensus must be exceptionally well specified and implemented.

Proposals follow one another on bitcoindev and Delving Bitcoin, relayed by Optech. On January 22, 2026, Giulio Golinelli proposed a fork to verify Falcon signatures, which would take up about 20 times more space than ECDSA, according to Optech, and verify about 2 times faster; Optech notes that implementing it would actually be a hard fork, contrary to what the message says. On January 25, 2026, Conduition's tests showed that SLH-DSA verification, parallelized on GPU, can match single-threaded Schnorr, and stays in the same order of magnitude as parallelized Schnorr. Optech relayed these two messages in its February 6 newsletter. An SLH-DSA signature, even optimized, remains on the order of 100 times slower than one on an elliptic curve, according to Optech in December 2025. On February 9, 2026, Ethan Heilman proposed cryptographic agility: a fallback algorithm provided for in P2MR scripts (Optech newsletter of March 6). On July 27, 2026, Pieter Wuille said he preferred deploying P2TRv2 with a hash-based post-quantum opcode, then an output type based on P2MR whose witness would allow the costs of elliptic curves and post-quantum signatures to be priced separately. On August 26, 2026, a draft BIP described SHRINCS, a semi-stateful hash-based signature (stateful signatures with a stateless fallback), with a 48-byte key and signatures of at least 548 bytes as long as the state is tracked, and 5,777 bytes in the stateless fallback.
