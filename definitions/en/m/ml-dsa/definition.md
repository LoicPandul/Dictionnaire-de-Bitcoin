Acronym for *Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm*. Post-quantum, lattice-based digital signature algorithm, standardized by NIST in FIPS 204 on August 13, 2024, based on CRYSTALS-Dilithium. Its security rests on MLWE, a structured variant of the LWE problem, and on SelfTargetMSIS, a non-standard variant of the SIS problem; NIST judges ML-DSA secure even against a large quantum computer. Its smallest variant, ML-DSA-44, produces 2,420-byte signatures for a 1,312-byte public key, and is the reference for several post-quantum signature proposals for Lightning, after having been the reference for BIP-0360 on Bitcoin.

ML-DSA works like a Schnorr signature in which numbers are replaced by vectors of polynomials. In a Schnorr signature, the signer commits to a random number, computes a challenge by hashing this commitment with the message, then responds by combining the secret, the challenge, and the randomness. ML-DSA follows these same 3 steps. Let's take an example. Alice draws a public matrix $A$, which she derives from a 256-bit seed, and 2 secrets $s_1$ and $s_2$ with small coefficients. Her public key contains the seed and the vector $t = A s_1 + s_2$, in compressed form. Recovering $s_1$ from $A$ and $t$ amounts to solving MLWE. To sign, Alice draws a vector $y$ with small coefficients and computes $w = A y$, of which she keeps only the high-order bits $w_1$ as the commitment. She hashes $w_1$ with the message to obtain a challenge $c$, then computes the response $z = y + s_1 c$. Bob, who knows the public key, checks that $z$ does have small coefficients, then reconstructs the commitment from $A z - t c$, with the help of a hint attached to the signature, and checks that it yields the same hash $c$.

This response has a flaw, however: left as is, $z$ leans in a direction tied to the secret $s_1$, and a series of signatures would eventually give it away. ML-DSA therefore applies rejection sampling (Fiat-Shamir with aborts): if a coefficient of $z$, or of the low-order part of $A y - c s_2$, falls outside a fixed range, Alice discards her attempt and starts over with a new $y$. The published signatures thus reveal nothing about the private key. For ML-DSA-44, NIST estimates that the signing loop runs about 4.25 times on average before producing a signature.

FIPS 204 defines 3 parameter sets, named after the dimensions of the matrix $A$:

* ML-DSA-44 (NIST security category 2): 1,312-byte public key, 2,420-byte signature;
* ML-DSA-65 (category 3): 1,952-byte public key, 3,309-byte signature;
* ML-DSA-87 (category 5): 2,592-byte public key, 4,627-byte signature.

The private key, of 2,560 to 4,896 bytes, can be reduced to a 32-byte seed from which it is rederived. The standard adds HashML-DSA, a variant that signs the hash of the message rather than the message itself. ML-DSA is designed to be strongly unforgeable: even after obtaining signatures on messages of its choice, an attacker cannot produce any other valid signature, not even a second signature on a message that has already been signed.

Compared with Bitcoin's current signatures, the size gap is clear. According to the Chaincode Labs report (May 2025), the ML-DSA-44 public key weighs 41 times a Schnorr key and its signature 38 times a 64-byte Schnorr signature; signing costs about 8 times more than with ECDSA, and verifying a little less. Compared with other post-quantum signatures, ML-DSA sits in between. An SLH-DSA-128s signature weighs 7,856 bytes, and a Falcon-512 signature (666 bytes), the future FN-DSA-512, is about 3.6 times smaller than an ML-DSA-44 one. FN-DSA, on the other hand, requires floating-point arithmetic that is tricky to run in constant time, unless it is emulated with integers, whereas ML-DSA, according to Cloudflare in 2022, is easier to implement safely and signs faster. Unlike the reduced variants of SLH-DSA, ML-DSA imposes no limit on the number of signatures from a single key.

ML-DSA held a central place in BIP-0360. Its version 0.3.0 (October 21, 2024) replaces XMSS with CRYSTALS-Dilithium there. The Chaincode report then describes a BIP-0360 that proposes 3 algorithms: FN-DSA-512, ML-DSA-44, and SLH-DSA-128s. In July 2025 (version 0.8.0), BIP-0360 already defers post-quantum signatures to a future BIP, which would target only 2 algorithms: ML-DSA as the main algorithm, and SLH-DSA, of radically different design, as a fallback; FN-DSA is set aside because it relies, like ML-DSA, on lattices, and would bring little diversity. This version considers dedicated opcodes such as `OP_CHECKMLSIG`. The December 2025 rewrite, which renames the proposal, then removes the detail of these signatures. As of October 25, 2026, BIP-0360 cites ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as examples to examine before any use, and no post-quantum signature is activated on Bitcoin.

On Lightning, ML-DSA appears as the natural candidate for off-chain signatures. In May 2026, on Delving Bitcoin, Olaoluwa Osuntokun compares ML-DSA-44 and SLH-DSA for BOLT-11 invoices and gossip announcements: on size alone, ML-DSA-44 wins, and SLH-DSA reduced to 2^24 signatures is unsuitable for gossip. He considers ML-DSA for off-chain signatures, SLH-DSA for on-chain ones, and ML-KEM for encrypting connections. He notes that a 64-byte Schnorr signature is about 3% of an ML-DSA-44 signature. In September 2026, the PQLN paper by Ahmet Kurt and his coauthors describes a hybrid extension of Lightning that adds ML-DSA-44 signatures to node announcements and to gossip channel updates (the channel announcement remains classical) and to invoices, implemented in a fork of rust-lightning: at initial sync, the gossip data becomes about 10 times larger.

CRYSTALS-Dilithium was among the candidates of NIST's post-quantum standardization from 2017. On July 5, 2022, NIST selected it along with Falcon and SPHINCS+, and recommended it as the primary signature algorithm. The FIPS 204 draft appeared on August 24, 2023, under the name ML-DSA, and the final version on August 13, 2024, the same day as FIPS 203 (ML-KEM) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA).

*The names CRYSTALS-Dilithium and Dilithium are still encountered. They refer to the scheme submitted to NIST, several details of which FIPS 204 modifies: ML-DSA is the name of the standardized version.*
