American cryptographer, coinventor of public-key cryptography and inventor of the hash tree that bears his name. His tree, which Bitcoin uses to summarize the transactions of a block, comes from his work as a doctoral student at Stanford.

Merkle submitted his first paper on public-key cryptography in 1974, and it was initially rejected. Diffie and Hellman cited his work in their 1976 paper, "New Directions in Cryptography." In June 1979, his Stanford doctoral thesis, "Secrecy, Authentication, and Public Key Systems," described the authentication tree: values are hashed in pairs, level by level, up to a single root, and proving that a value belongs to the tree requires only the path of hashes up to that root. U.S. Patent 4,309,569, "Method of providing digital signatures," filed on September 5, 1979, in Merkle's name and issued on January 5, 1982, applies this tree to the digital signature. In April 1980, he published "Protocols for public key cryptosystems" at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy.

This paper is reference [7] of the Bitcoin white paper. The white paper points to it (along with references [2] and [5]) in the section on reclaiming disk space: the transactions of a block are hashed in a Merkle tree, of which only the root goes into the block's hash, which makes it possible to prune old branches. This is the Merkle root. The next section relies on the same structure: a user who keeps only block headers (simplified payment verification, SPV) verifies a transaction with its Merkle branch alone. In 1993, Dave Bayer, Stuart Haber, and W. Scott Stornetta published a paper (circulated as early as March 1992) that reuses Merkle's hash trees for timestamping.
