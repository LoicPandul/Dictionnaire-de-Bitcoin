Acronym for *Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism*. Post-quantum, lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism, standardized by NIST in FIPS 203 on August 13, 2024, based on CRYSTALS-Kyber. It lets 2 parties establish a shared secret over a public channel, but signs nothing: it is the post-quantum alternative to ECDH-style key exchange, not to ECDSA or Schnorr. Its security rests on MLWE, a structured variant of the LWE problem, which no verified quantum algorithm solves efficiently to date.

A key encapsulation mechanism (KEM) runs in 3 operations. Let's take an example. Alice generates a key pair: a decapsulation key, which she keeps private, and an encapsulation key, which she publishes. Bob uses Alice's encapsulation key to produce both a 32-byte shared secret and a ciphertext, which he sends to Alice. Alice applies her decapsulation key to the ciphertext and recovers the same secret. A passive observer who sees the encapsulation key and the ciphertext go by cannot, with known computing means, deduce the secret. Alice and Bob then use this secret with symmetric encryption.

This mechanism resembles a Diffie-Hellman exchange without being one. In ECDH, each party combines its own private key with the other's public key to obtain the same secret: the computation is symmetric. If each already knows the other's static key, no exchange is needed. In a KEM, Alice's encapsulation key only serves to encrypt a secret for her: to establish a key, Bob must reply to her with a message, the ciphertext. No post-quantum scheme selected by NIST offers a non-interactive equivalent of Diffie-Hellman, and according to Olaoluwa Osuntokun, ML-KEM, which in fact encrypts a secret for Alice with a lattice key, looks more like public-key encryption than like a key exchange in the strict sense. ECDH, on the other hand, can be seen as a particular KEM, when the two messages are sent one after the other. The two can then be substituted in protocols written for this interface, but not in those that require a non-interactive exchange.

The computation takes place on polynomials with 256 coefficients modulo the prime number 3329. The encapsulation key contains a vector of such polynomials, of the form $A s + e$ with $s$ and $e$ having small coefficients, which MLWE, under the accepted assumption, makes impossible to decompose efficiently without the secret $s$. The ciphertext hides the secret behind noise of the same kind. FIPS 203 defines 3 parameter sets:

* ML-KEM-512 (NIST security category 1): 800-byte encapsulation key, 768-byte ciphertext;
* ML-KEM-768 (category 3): 1,184-byte encapsulation key, 1,088-byte ciphertext;
* ML-KEM-1024 (category 5): 1,568-byte encapsulation key, 1,568-byte ciphertext.

The decapsulation key weighs 1,632 to 3,168 bytes, and the shared secret is always 32 bytes. Because noise can occasionally distort the result, decapsulation yields, with a tiny probability, a secret different from the one produced by encapsulation (decapsulation failure), from $2^{-138.8}$ for ML-KEM-512 to $2^{-174.8}$ for ML-KEM-1024.

The need is pressing even before a CRQC exists. An adversary can record encrypted exchanges today and decrypt them later, once a quantum computer is available: this is the harvest now, decrypt later attack. Against it, signatures matter less than key establishment. The Chaincode Labs report (May 2025) recalls that Cloudflare deployed a hybrid key exchange combining an elliptic curve and Kyber in October 2022, that Chrome followed in August 2023, and that Signal added CRYSTALS-Kyber in September 2023.

On Bitcoin, the most concrete proposal concerns the encryption of connections between nodes. BIP-0324 establishes this encryption with an ECDH exchange on secp256k1, whose keys are encoded with ElligatorSwift to look like random bytes. On May 4, 2026, on the bitcoindev mailing list, Osuntokun points out that a quantum computer could recover the ephemeral keys and decrypt recorded traffic. Changing this protocol requires no change to the consensus rules: each pair of nodes freely agrees on the transport version. He lays out 2 options: a hybrid exchange, which keeps ECDH and adds ML-KEM, secure as long as either one holds, or pure ML-KEM, with 1,184-byte ML-KEM-768 encapsulation keys (about 1.2 kB). An ML-KEM key is not indistinguishable from randomness, since its coefficients stay below 3329, which loses the discretion that ElligatorSwift offers; Kemeleon, an encoding that aims to make these keys look random, or an upgrade through ML-KEM inside the already encrypted ECDH channel, are proposed to restore it. Jonas Schnelli prefers the second route on May 6, 2026. As of October 2026, no BIP draft specifies the ML-KEM exchange; only a framework for negotiating transport features, which would prepare this upgrade, was proposed on September 18.

On Lightning, the same author examines in May 2026 on Delving Bitcoin what ML-KEM would change. The BOLT-08 connections would require a KEM key announced in `node_announcement` (BOLT-07), in addition to the node key. The BOLT-04 onions, today built on a single curve point blinded from hop to hop, would have to contain an ML-KEM ciphertext of about 1 kB per hop. With 20 hops in ML-KEM-768, `update_add_htlc` would grow from 1,450 to 23,177 bytes, about 16 times larger; according to the author, the median route length would be 4 to 6 hops, which mitigates this cost. The PQLN paper (September 2026) picks ML-KEM-768 for a hybrid handshake and hybrid onions. ML-KEM and ML-DSA, although built on the same module lattices, use different keys: an encapsulation key does not sign.

CRYSTALS-Kyber was one of the candidates of NIST's post-quantum standardization. On July 5, 2022, NIST selected it for general encryption, because of its relatively small keys and its speed. The FIPS 203 draft, under the name ML-KEM, appeared on August 24, 2023, and the final version on August 13, 2024, the same day as FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA).

*The name CRYSTALS-Kyber, or Kyber, is still encountered: it is the name of the scheme submitted to NIST. ML-KEM is the name of the standardized version, derived from Kyber with a few modifications that make it incompatible with the round-three version.*
