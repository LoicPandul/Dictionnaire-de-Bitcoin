Economic principle stating that “bad money drives out good”. When two currencies circulate at the same time with a legal tender rate imposed, economic agents tend to hoard the one that keeps its value and to spend the one that loses value first. The lower-quality money then dominates exchange, while the superior money disappears from circulation.

The phenomenon was formulated by the English financier Thomas Gresham in 1558, in a letter to Queen Elizabeth I, to describe the debased state of English silver coins resulting from the Great Debasement carried out under Henry VIII and Edward VI (1544-1551). It has historically been observed whenever a state imposes an artificial parity between two forms of money: coins with a high precious-metal content are melted down, exported, or hoarded, while debased coins keep circulating.

In the context of Bitcoin, some invoke Gresham's law to explain why bitcoiners prefer to spend fiat currencies, seen as losing value, rather than their bitcoin, seen as gaining value. In this reading, bitcoin plays the role of the hoarded “good money”, and fiat currencies are spent first, which reinforces bitcoin's role as a store of value at the expense of its use as a means of payment.

However, this application is contested. Strictly speaking, Gresham's law assumes a legal tender rate or a fixed exchange rate between the two currencies, a condition absent between bitcoin and fiat currencies, which trade freely on markets. Moreover, other, more prosaic factors explain why bitcoin is rarely spent: in many jurisdictions, every spend of bitcoin is a taxable event subject to capital gains tax, which discourages everyday use; also, the number of merchants accepting bitcoin remains very limited, making it simply impractical to pay with.
