Acronym for *Fast-Fourier Transform over NTRU-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm*. Post-quantum digital signature algorithm based on NTRU lattices, derived from Falcon, which NIST is preparing as the FIPS 206 standard. It is the most compact of the post-quantum signatures selected by NIST: a Falcon-512 signature weighs 666 bytes for a public key of 897 bytes, against 2,420 and 1,312 bytes for ML-DSA-44. Signing, which relies on floating-point computations, is on the other hand difficult to implement safely.

FN-DSA follows a different construction from ML-DSA, known as "hash-and-sign." ML-DSA transposes the Schnorr signature to lattices: commitment, challenge, response. FN-DSA, for its part, takes up the framework proposed by Gentry, Peikert, and Vaikuntanathan and applies it to NTRU lattices, lattices endowed with an algebraic structure that shortens keys. Alice's private key is a "trapdoor": a short basis of the lattice, which makes it easy to find points close to a target. Her public key describes the same lattice by a basis that does not provide this shortcut. Let's take an example. To sign a message, Alice hashes it, derives a target point from the hash, then uses her trapdoor to find a lattice point very close to this target; the signature is the short difference between the two, accompanied by a random salt (Falcon transmits only half of this difference, and the other half is recomputed). Bob verifies, with the public key alone, that this difference is indeed short and that it links the hash to a lattice point. Without the trapdoor, producing such a short difference amounts to solving the SIS problem on NTRU lattices, which no one knows how to solve efficiently in the general case, even with a quantum computer.

The delicate point is how to choose this nearby point. If Alice computed it by a deterministic method, each signature would leak a little information about her secret basis, until it could be reconstructed: this is how GGH and NTRUSign, two of the first lattice-based signatures, were broken. Falcon therefore draws this point at random according to a discrete Gaussian distribution, using a sampler based on the fast Fourier transform (*fast Fourier sampling*), which gives the standard its name. According to its designers, this sampling leaks only negligible information about the private key, at least up to $2^{64}$ signatures, the limit set by NIST.

This sampler is both the strength and the weakness of the scheme. It works in double-precision floating point, which makes signing fast: some 5,900 signatures and 28,000 verifications per second for Falcon-512 on a desktop processor, according to measurements published by the Falcon team. But these computations must run in constant time, because if the duration of a signature depends on the private key, measuring that time often makes it possible to recover the key. Cloudflare, which in 2022 judged Falcon "the best of the worst" for size, points out that it is the first major cryptographic algorithm to rely on this type of computation, which many processors do not execute quickly in constant time (without a floating-point unit, it is emulated with integers), and reserves it for signatures produced offline, such as those of certificates. The IETF draft on X.509 certificates for FN-DSA specifies that FN-DSA offers only randomized signing, because a floating-point implementation error could produce different signatures for the same hash. Power-consumption analyses have also already been demonstrated against Falcon implementations (Guerreau et al., 2022). Verification does not have this problem.

The standard provides for 2 levels:

* FN-DSA-512: public key of 897 bytes, signature of about 666 bytes;
* FN-DSA-1024: public key of 1,793 bytes, signature of about 1,280 bytes.

According to the Chaincode Labs report (May 2025), a Falcon-512 signature weighs 10 times a Schnorr signature and its public key 28 times a Schnorr key; with Falcon-512, signing costs about 24 times more than with ECDSA, and verifying costs about 0.6 times as much. The same draft specifies that FN-DSA and Falcon are not compatible.

On Bitcoin, FN-DSA was first the favorite, then set aside. In January 2025, BIP-0360 provided for introducing Falcon signatures first, noting that they weigh about 20 times a Schnorr signature. In July 2025 (version 0.8.0), the proposal already deferred post-quantum signatures to a future BIP, which would select ML-DSA as the main signature and SLH-DSA as a backup, and would set aside FN-DSA because, also based on lattices, it would bring little diversity of assumptions compared with ML-DSA, while acknowledging that the 2 schemes rely on different lattice assumptions. The December 2025 rewrite then removed the details of these signatures from the text of BIP-0360.

On January 21, 2026, on the bitcoindev list, Giulio Golinelli presented a fork of Bitcoin Core that integrates Falcon-512 into the P2WPKH spending path, and measured a verification time of 57 µs against 120 µs for ECDSA, with signatures of 655 bytes. The Optech newsletter of February 6, 2026 notes that this implementation, presented as a soft fork, actually appears to be a hard fork: it substitutes Falcon verification for ECDSA verification in the P2WPKH path, on a "flag day" basis. In the same thread, Conduition explains that floating-point Gaussian sampling, very difficult to implement both fast and in constant time, brings only a gain of about a factor of 2 on the combined size of signature and key compared with ML-DSA, and that FN-DSA will probably not appear in the post-quantum signature opcode BIP that is to follow BIP-0360. In May 2026, on the bitcoindev list, Mikhail Kudinov noted that Falcon's floating-point computations can be simulated with integers, at the cost of lower efficiency.

Falcon was submitted to NIST on November 30, 2017 by a team of 10 cryptographers, including Pierre-Alain Fouque, Thomas Pornin, and Thomas Prest. On July 5, 2022, NIST selected it, recommending Dilithium as the main algorithm and Falcon for applications that need smaller signatures. Falcon is to be published in FIPS 206 under the name FN-DSA. As of October 2026, this standard is still in preparation: NIST's standardization project page describes it as under development.

*FN-DSA designates the version of Falcon being standardized, and the 2 are not interchangeable. The names Falcon-512 and Falcon-1024 are also used for the 2 security levels.*
