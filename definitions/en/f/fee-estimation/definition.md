Calculation of the fee rate (in sat/vB) that a transaction must pay to have a good chance of being confirmed within a given number of blocks, the confirmation target. Miners select first the transactions that pay the most per unit of virtual size, and that price varies from block to block with demand: a wallet cannot know it in advance and has to estimate it from what its node has observed.

Bitcoin Core's historical estimator, called *block policy*, reasons from the past. It assumes that, for equal size, a transaction paying a higher rate is mined before one paying less. Each eligible transaction entering the mempool is filed, along with its arrival height, in a bucket of neighboring rates: the buckets range from 0.1 sat/vB (1 sat/vB before 31.0) to about 9,500 sat/vB, plus a last bucket with no ceiling, each 5% higher than the previous one. When a block arrives, the node counts how many blocks each transaction took to be mined. To answer for a target, it starts from the highest bucket, moves down through groups of buckets that hold enough transactions, and keeps the lowest group that reaches the required proportion of transactions confirmed within the target. The estimate is the average rate of the median bucket of that group. Only transactions with no unconfirmed parent in the mempool and outside packages count; those that leave the mempool without being mined count as failures for the targets that had already elapsed when they left.

The statistics are exponentially decaying averages over 3 horizons: short (half-life of 18 blocks, about 3 hours, targets up to 12 blocks), medium (144 blocks, about 1 day, up to 48), and long (1,008 blocks, about 1 week, up to 1,008). The estimate keeps the highest of three rates: the one that succeeds at 60% for half the target, at 85% for the target, and at 95% for twice the target. Bitcoin Core treats a target of 1 block as a target of 2, for lack of a reliable estimate at 1 block, and caps the target at half the number of blocks observed: a new node may therefore return nothing. The statistics are saved to disk to survive restarts, in `fee_estimates.dat` up to Bitcoin Core 31 and in `fees/block_policy_estimates.dat` since 32.0.

The `estimatesmartfee` RPC offers 2 modes. The conservative mode also requires the 95% threshold at twice the target to hold over the longer horizons, which prevents a temporary lull from pulling the estimate down. The economical mode follows the short term more closely: it can return a much lower rate during a quiet weekend, at the risk of leaving the transaction pending if fees rise again. The conservative mode was the RPC default in Bitcoin Core 0.15.0; since 28.0, the default is the economical mode, because the conservative one often led to overpaying. In Bitcoin Core's wallet, the default target is 6 blocks. The estimated rate is raised to the mempool minimum and then to the required minimum rate, the higher of the wallet's `-mintxfee` option (1 sat/vB by default) and the minimum relay rate (see MINRELAYTXFEE). If the estimator lacks data, the wallet falls back to the `-fallbackfee` option, whose default value is 0, which disables this fallback.

Let's take an example. Alice wants to pay Bob with a 140 vB transaction, to be confirmed within the next 6 blocks. Her wallet queries her node, which answers 8 sat/vB, or 1,120 sats in fees. If the fee market then rises, Alice can replace her transaction with a more expensive version (see RBF - REPLACE-BY-FEE) or have a child transaction spend it (see CPFP - CHILD PAYS FOR PARENT).

The historical estimator reacts slowly: it updates only once per block. In return, it resists manipulation, since it retains only transactions relayed to all miners, and those that fail to confirm count as failures. It does not predict the future, it describes what happened recently. In late 2023, BTCPay Server switched its default to mempool.space estimates, with a fallback to the local node, because developers judged Bitcoin Core's estimates too slow to follow the market; since version 2.4.6, released on October 26, 2026, it queries its Bitcoin Core node first and falls back to mempool.space only when that fails. LND, for its part, does not rely on a single estimate: it asks an external estimator for a rate for the initial rate and for non-urgent transactions, then increases fees linearly at each block, until the whole budget of the transaction goes to fees when the deadline is only one block away.

On March 21, 2024, Abubakar Sadiq Ismail proposed on Delving Bitcoin to estimate the rate by building, from the node's mempool, the block that the node would mine, then reading the rate at the 50th percentile of that block's weight. The method assumes that the node's mempool resembles the miners'. A different relay policy, a poor connection, or transactions sent to miners outside the peer-to-peer network skew it, and it loses reliability beyond a target of 1 block. Bitcoin Core 32.0 integrates this idea (PR 34075) with a second estimator, which builds a block template with the node's block-building algorithm and reads the rate of the slice of transactions that reaches 50% of a block's maximum weight for the conservative estimate, 75% for the economical one. It answers only if the transactions removed from the mempool for the last 6 mined blocks weigh at least 75% of the weight of those blocks, except when those 6 blocks weigh less than one full block in total, in which case the check is skipped; otherwise the historical estimator answers alone. By default, `estimatesmartfee` returns the lower of the 2 estimates: the mempool can lower the rate, never raise it. The `fee_rate_estimator` option lets the user choose `auto`, `block_policy`, or `mempool_policy`.

Bitcoin Core's bucket-based estimator is the work of Alex Morcos: its first version entered Bitcoin Core 0.11.0, the `estimatesmartfee` RPC arrived in 0.12.0, and its rewrite over 3 horizons, with the conservative and economical modes, in 0.15.0.

*Not to be confused with the estimates file (`fee_estimates.dat`, then `fees/block_policy_estimates.dat`), which keeps only its statistics, nor with the minimum relay rate, a relay-policy threshold that says nothing about the market price.*
