Technique that consists of making several fictitious transactions to oneself in order to simulate a transfer of ownership of bitcoin. Ricochet blurs the specific traits that can compromise the fungibility of a Bitcoin UTXO. For example, after a coinjoin, the UTXO coming out of the mix is identified as such. This "coinjoin UTXO" label can affect the fungibility of the UTXO. Regulated entities, such as exchanges, may refuse to accept a UTXO that went through a coinjoin, or even demand explanations from its owner, at the risk of the account being blocked or the funds frozen. In some cases, the exchange may even report this behavior to government authorities. This is where the Ricochet method comes in. To blur the footprint left by a coinjoin, Ricochet executes four successive transactions in which the user sends their funds to themselves on different addresses. After this chain of transactions, the Ricochet tool finally routes the bitcoin to its final destination, such as an exchange. The goal is to create distance between the original coinjoin transaction and the final act of spending. This way, chain analysis tools will likely conclude that a transfer of ownership took place after the coinjoin, and that there is therefore no reason to take action against the sender. The most common use case for Ricochet arises when a past coinjoin participation on a UTXO needs to be concealed, notably to avoid being targeted by the AML/CFT policies of regulated platforms or by blacklists. The Ricochet tool was offered by Samourai Wallet, whose domain name and servers were seized in April 2024.
