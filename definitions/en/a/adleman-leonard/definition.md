American computer scientist and mathematician, coinventor with Ron Rivest and Adi Shamir of RSA, the first published public-key encryption and signature system usable in practice. Born on December 31, 1945, in San Francisco, he earned a PhD in computer science from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1976, with a thesis on number-theoretic aspects of computational complexity.

From 1976 to 1980, he taught mathematics at MIT. There he worked with Rivest and Shamir on the public-key cryptography that Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman had just described in November 1976, and for which an efficient implementation of encryption was still missing. The trio published “A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems” in 1978. RSA, named after the initials of their surnames, draws its security from the presumed difficulty of factoring very large integers: Alice publishes her public key, Bob uses it to encrypt a message to her or to verify a signature, and only Alice, who holds the private key, can decrypt that message or produce that signature.

In 1980, Adleman joined the University of Southern California (USC), where he later held the Henry Salvatori Chair in Computer Science. There he extended his research to biology and is regarded as the father of DNA computing. In 2002, he received the Turing Award with Rivest and Shamir, “for their ingenious contribution to making public-key cryptography useful in practice.”

Bitcoin does not use RSA: its keys rely on the secp256k1 elliptic curve and its signatures on ECDSA, joined by the Schnorr scheme with Taproot. RSA remains the first concrete implementation of the digital signature, the notion that lets a bitcoin owner authorize a spend without revealing the private key.
