Public process by which NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the US federal agency in charge of measurements, standards, and technology) chooses and publishes the cryptographic algorithms meant to resist a quantum computer. It resembles an international competition: teams of researchers submit algorithms, the community tries to break them, then NIST selects those it judges best suited and turns them into federal standards, the FIPS (Federal Information Processing Standards). These standards serve as the reference for post-quantum cryptography, and many proposals for Bitcoin build on them, even though some explore other schemes.

NIST officially launched the process on December 20, 2016, by publishing a call for submissions of post-quantum public-key algorithms. On July 5, 2022, it selected four algorithms: CRYSTALS-Kyber for encryption, and three signature algorithms, CRYSTALS-Dilithium, FALCON, and SPHINCS+. It recommended Dilithium as the primary signature algorithm, FALCON for cases that need smaller signatures, and SPHINCS+ as a fallback, because that algorithm relies on hash functions whereas the other two rely on lattices: if a flaw affected the latter family, SPHINCS+ would remain intact. Four other encryption algorithms remained under study at that point.

On August 13, 2024, NIST published the first three finalized standards. FIPS 203 defines ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber, a key encapsulation mechanism used to establish a shared secret. FIPS 204 defines ML-DSA, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium, and FIPS 205 defines SLH-DSA, derived from SPHINCS+, two digital signatures. According to NIST, these three standards will provide the basis for most post-quantum cryptography deployments, and they can be used right away. The fourth, FIPS 206, is to standardize FN-DSA, the name given to FALCON: in October 2026, NIST's page describes it as under development, and no draft could be found.

The process continues on two tracks. For encryption, NIST selected HQC on March 11, 2025, an algorithm based on error-correcting codes, which is meant to serve as a backup to ML-KEM in case a flaw is discovered in lattices. It then planned a draft standard about a year later and a final standard in 2027. For signatures, a call for additional signatures targets algorithms that could supplement ML-DSA or meet particular needs. It has 14 candidates in the second round, and NIST's page, updated on October 13, 2026, presents a third round that includes SQIsign, a signature based on isogenies of elliptic curves.

NIST also frames the replacement of existing algorithms. Its report NIST IR 8547, whose initial draft dates from November 12, 2024, provides for its own standards that ECDSA and RSA at 112 bits of security be deprecated after 2030, and that they be disallowed after 2035, like ECDSA, EdDSA, and RSA at 128 bits of security and above. These deadlines are the ones NIST sets for the algorithms in its standards, which US federal agencies apply; the report is still only a draft. They are not binding on Bitcoin, which depends on no standards authority (NIST does, incidentally, accept the secp256k1 curve for blockchain-type applications), and the report does not mention the Schnorr signature of BIP-0340.

For Bitcoin, NIST standardization provides algorithms already published as standards, which avoids having to choose one from scratch. BIP-0360 thus replaces XMSS with CRYSTALS-Dilithium in its version 0.3.0 of October 21, 2024, because of NIST's approval and size constraints; its current text names ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as examples of algorithms to examine before use, without settling on one. The choice remains open: these signatures offer different levels of protection and are much larger than Schnorr's (from 2,420 to 49,856 bytes, against 64); see ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for the sizes. The migration that these standards would make possible is described in the entry on post-quantum migration.

*NIST files this process under the acronym PQC, which refers to post-quantum cryptography in general.*
