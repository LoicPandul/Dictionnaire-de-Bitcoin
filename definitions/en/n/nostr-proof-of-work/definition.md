Anti-spam mechanism defined by NIP-13, in which the author of a Nostr event varies a counter in a `nonce` tag until the event ID starts with a chosen number of zero bits. The principle is that of proof of work in Bitcoin mining, applied to a message.

An event ID is the `SHA256` of its serialization. Difficulty is measured as the number of leading zero bits of this ID. An ID that starts with `000000000e9d…` has a difficulty of 36, one that starts with `002f…` a difficulty of 10: `002f` is written `0000 0000 0010 1111` in binary, and if the first nonzero digit is less than or equal to 7, its leading zeros count too. Each additional zero bit required doubles the number of attempts on average.

To mine a note, the author adds a tag `["nonce", "<counter>", "<target difficulty>"]`, changes the counter, recomputes the ID, and starts over until the desired difficulty is reached. NIP-13 advises also updating `created_at` during this work. The target difficulty, the third value of the tag, should be given: it lets the client discard a spammer who aimed for a low difficulty and landed by chance on a higher one. A client that requires 40 bits can reject a note that announces 30, even if its ID has 40, and can also reject any note without this commitment.

Let's take an example. A relay announces in its NIP-11 information document a `min_pow_difficulty` of 30. Alice publishes a note: her device mines it until it obtains an ID with 30 or more zero bits. Relays check with a single hash that the ID starts with enough zeros, without redoing the mining, and accept the note. A note that does not reach the threshold receives in response an `OK` message of the form "pow: difficulty 26 is less than 30". A spammer who wanted to flood the relay would have to pay for this computation for every note.

The difference from Bitcoin is large. No chain links the proofs together, no block is rewarded, and the difficulty is not adjusted: each relay that requires one sets its own threshold, and the author redoes the work for each of their events. Because the ID does not depend on the signature, the computation can be outsourced to a provider, possibly a paid one, which spares a phone from computing it itself. NIP-17 private messages go through random keys, so relays cannot apply anti-spam based on a key's reputation to them: NIP-59 recommends NIP-42 authentication instead, and leaves clients free to add a proof of work to the envelope.

NIP-13, written by William Casarin, was added to the NIPs repository by fiatjaf on May 6, 2022, and remains at `draft` status. A section on searching by proof-of-work prefix was removed from it in August 2024.
