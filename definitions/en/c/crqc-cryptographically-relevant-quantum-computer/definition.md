Acronym for *Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer*. Quantum computer able to break the public-key cryptography in use within a reasonable time, and in particular to recover a private key from the corresponding public key. The term is defined by a capability threshold, whatever the technology of the machine: once a CRQC exists, ECDSA and Schnorr signatures no longer guarantee that only the holder of the private key can spend bitcoin whose public key is known. No CRQC is known as of 2026.

This threshold is defined relative to Shor's algorithm. Bitcoin's ECDSA and Schnorr signatures both rely on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem on the secp256k1 elliptic curve: computing a public key from a private key is nearly instantaneous on a personal computer, whereas the reverse operation takes a classical computer about $2^{128}$ operations, which puts it out of reach. Devised in 1994 by Peter Shor, Shor's algorithm solves this problem exponentially faster on a quantum computer. In the context of Bitcoin, a CRQC is therefore a machine able to run this algorithm efficiently against the 256-bit keys of secp256k1: according to the Chaincode Labs report of May 2025, it could recover a private key in a few hours to a few days. The same attack works against RSA and Diffie-Hellman key exchanges, so a CRQC would also threaten most of the cryptographic systems of the Internet that had not migrated.

The size of a CRQC is measured in logical qubits, then in physical qubits once error correction is taken into account. Physical qubits are error-prone; error correction combines several of them to obtain one reliable logical qubit, at a rate of 10 to 1,000 physical qubits per logical qubit according to the Chaincode report. In 2017, Martin Roetteler, Michael Naehrig, Krysta Svore, and Kristin Lauter estimated that the discrete logarithm on an elliptic curve defined over a 256-bit prime field, like secp256k1, requires at most 2,330 logical qubits and about 126 billion Toffoli gates; their simulation covers NIST's P-256 curve, which has the same size. Google's Willow processor, announced in December 2024, has 105 physical qubits. BIP-0360 also counts in logical qubits, describing a CRQC as a machine, whatever its hardware, able to keep enough logical qubits coherent to run Shor's algorithm efficiently. The exact threshold remains poorly defined: the Swiss Federal Office for Cybersecurity wrote in 2024 that the size at which a quantum computer becomes a CRQC "is not settled today".

A CRQC first threatens the public keys already visible on the blockchain. Let's take an example. Alice holds 1 BTC on a P2TR output: this output contains her Taproot output key, a public key readable by everyone as long as the funds do not move. An attacker equipped with a CRQC copies this key, runs Shor's algorithm for a few hours or days, obtains the corresponding private key, and signs a transaction that sends Alice's bitcoin to an address the attacker controls. Nodes accept this transaction, since its signature is valid. BIP-0360 calls this scenario a long-exposure attack: the attacker has all the time in the world as long as the funds remain on the output. Had Alice received her bitcoin on a P2WPKH address that was never reused, only the hash of her public key would be visible. The key would appear only at spending time, and the attacker would have to break it while the transaction waits for confirmation in the mempool. This short-exposure attack requires a faster CRQC, and BIP-0360 considers it unlikely that the first CRQCs will be capable of it.

Published estimates vary widely depending on the architecture considered. In 2023, Daniel Litinski calculated that a hypothetical machine of 6,000 modules of 1,152 physical qubits, linked together by nonlocal connections, would produce a 256-bit private key every 10 minutes, the average interval targeted between two Bitcoin blocks. A machine whose qubits only interact with their neighbors on a grid would need more qubits and would produce only one key every 38 hours. These figures remain estimates, which depend on assumptions about the error rate and the speed of future processors.

The amount of bitcoin exposed depends on the script type and on user habits. According to BIP-0360 and the Chaincode report, P2PK outputs (including Satoshi's coins), P2MS, and P2TR, as well as reused addresses, are vulnerable to a long-exposure attack. P2PKH, P2SH, P2WPKH, and P2WSH outputs become vulnerable only once their public key is revealed, and sharing an extended public key (xpub) or a descriptor also exposes public keys. BIP-0361 estimates that as of March 1, 2026, more than 34% of bitcoin had a public key revealed on the blockchain; the Chaincode report, in May 2025, put the potentially vulnerable funds between 4 and 10 million BTC, or 20 to 50% of the bitcoin in circulation.

The CRQC threat concerns public-key cryptography, and hash functions much less. Against `SHA256` and `RIPEMD160`, Grover's algorithm provides only a quadratic speedup, and the Chaincode report judges that breaking them would take a quantum computer well beyond any CRQC foreseeable in the short and medium term. Mining is also less exposed: a single machine is enough to steal funds whose public key is known, whereas mining with quantum computers would require a fleet of fast machines to compete with ASICs, and this computation parallelizes poorly. Outside signatures, the ECDH key exchange that opens the encrypted connections between nodes defined by BIP-0324 can also be broken by a CRQC: in May 2026, Olaoluwa Osuntokun proposed to complement or replace it with ML-KEM, a post-quantum key encapsulation mechanism.

The NSA wrote in 2021 that it does not know when a CRQC will exist, or even whether one will, and BIP-0360 makes the same observation. The Chaincode report places the first possible appearance of a CRQC able to break Bitcoin's elliptic curve cryptography between 2030 and 2035, based on a survey in which nearly a third of the 32 experts polled give it at least even odds. Governments have already set their deadlines: the White House memorandum NSM-10 (May 2022) aims to mitigate by 2035 as much as possible of the quantum risk to US cryptographic systems, and the draft NIST IR 8547 (November 2024) plans to disallow ECDSA after 2035. The resources required fall as algorithms improve: in 2019, Craig Gidney and Martin Ekerå estimated that 20 million noisy qubits were needed to factor a 2,048-bit RSA key in 8 hours; in 2025, Craig Gidney brought this figure down to under one million qubits, for less than a week of computation. Finally, BIP-0361 points out that an attacker could keep their machine secret and defer their thefts, so that Q-day, the day a quantum computer becomes capable of breaking these signatures, might only be known much later.

These uncertainties feed the proposals for a post-quantum migration of Bitcoin. BIP-0360 proposes P2MR, an output type close to P2TR but without a key path spend, which resists long-exposure attacks without waiting for post-quantum signatures, as long as its public keys are not reused. BIP-0361, submitted in 2025 by Jameson Lopp and his coauthors and merged into the BIPs repository in 2026, describes a series of soft forks that begins well before the possible arrival of a CRQC and progressively restricts spending by ECDSA and Schnorr signatures.

The acronym comes from the vocabulary of cybersecurity agencies: according to ANSSI, the UK's NCSC introduced it in a white paper of November 2020. In August 2021, the NSA's FAQ on quantum computing defined it as a quantum computer capable of actually attacking cryptographic systems that a classical computer cannot attack. The term was then picked up by NIST, then in Bitcoin discussions: BIP-0360 and the Chaincode Labs report use it routinely, BIP-0361 and the Bitcoin Optech newsletters more occasionally.

*The same acronym is also expanded as "Cryptanalytically Relevant Quantum Computer", the form used by the White House memorandum NSM-10.*
