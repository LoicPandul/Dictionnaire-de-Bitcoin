Two-step spending method in which the user first publishes a commitment, tied to their transaction or, depending on the variant, to the key that will authorize it, without revealing what proves they own it, then, once that commitment is confirmed, reveals this secret and what is needed to authorize the spend. Proposed for Bitcoin as a rescue protocol against a quantum computer, it aims to make an attacker who discovers the secret arrive too late: in the variants that order commitments, the transaction the attacker would like to substitute for the user's was not committed before the user's.

The mechanism is a variant of the Guy Fawkes signature, described by Ross Anderson, Francesco Bergadano, Bruno Crispo, Jong-Hyeon Lee, Charalampos Manifavas, and Roger Needham in the paper *A New Family of Authentication Protocols* (ACM SIGOPS Operating Systems Review, 1998). The authors say the idea came to them on November 4, 1996, while discussing how a modern Guy Fawkes, about to blow up Parliament, could publicize his cause without getting caught, hence the name. At each round, a hash is first published that binds the message, the current password, and the hash of the next one, and then these values are revealed. The commitment gives the signature its non-repudiable character; the reveal proves knowledge of the password. In this protocol, all secrets eventually become public. Joseph Bonneau and Andrew Miller derive from it Fawkescoin, a currency with no public-key cryptography whose transfers take two messages: a commitment, then, a few blocks later, the finalization that reveals the secret.

The principle is easiest to see in an example adapted from Tadge Dryja's proposal (May 28, 2025). Alice has bitcoin on a hashed address, whose public key has not yet appeared anywhere on the chain. A quantum computer could recover the private key from a visible public key, and Alice's will become visible when she broadcasts her transaction. She therefore starts by publishing, in an `OP_RETURN` output, a commitment made of three values: the hash of her public key, a hash of that key and of the ID of her spending transaction (which cannot be computed without knowing the public key), and the transaction ID. She waits until this commitment is confirmed to a sufficient depth, then broadcasts the transaction, which reveals her public key. A quantum attacker sees the key in the mempool and can derive the private key from it, but can no longer replace the transaction: nodes keep all the commitments for an address until the key is revealed, then keep only the oldest valid one, and Alice's is already in the chain.

The variants differ in what is committed and in how the honest commitment is protected: rejecting fraudulent commitments, or imposing a delay. Tim Ruffing published the first on January 24, 2018 on bitcoin-dev, then, on January 26, gave a version titled “Recovery of old UTXOs in a post-quantum world”, which commits to a message authentication code of the transaction computed with the public key. He himself acknowledged a flaw on February 15, 2018: if the public key is already revealed, anyone can prevent the spend by inserting the first commitment with an arbitrary transaction. His corrected variant applies to hash-based addresses whose preimage has not been revealed, and commits to the transaction encrypted with a key derived from that secret. Dryja replaces this encryption with a smaller hash commitment (96 bytes, or 48 once truncated) and describes it as a soft fork, which would activate once a “proof of a quantum computer” is observed: the confirmed spend of a script that only a quantum computer (or another method that breaks secp256k1) could satisfy. His document *Lifeboat and Lifejacket*, filed on GitHub on August 27, 2026, reuses this commitment for public keys that have never been seen (Lifeboat) and extends it to those that have (Lifejacket), provided the user can prove a precursor of their public key, such as a `SHA256` preimage or a BIP-0032 derivation. On August 20, 2026, conduition sketched DropKick: the commitment, hidden in a block, covers a post-quantum public key and a witness, which the user reveals later with a proof that the commitment appears in a sufficiently old block. Aggregators can bundle the commitments of several users, and a delay imposed by consensus separates the commitment from the reveal, at least 100 blocks in the example where the user agrees to give up 1% of their output to miners.

The scheme has limits. The basic scheme does not protect outputs whose public key is already visible, which Lifejacket covers only if the user holds a precursor of that key. For P2PK, DropKick and BIP-0361 judge that no rescue protocol is possible, for lack of a secret the attacker would not know. A deep chain reorganization that canceled the commitment would allow theft. Anyone can flood the chain with random commitments, which forces nodes to store them; Ruffing finds it rather ugly to require fees from someone whose only funds are these, while seeing it as a possible price of recovery. In DropKick, nodes do not retain all the commitments and therefore cannot know which is the oldest: security then rests on a long delay, on a share of the output paid to miners so that they prefer not to censor the reveal, and on the absence of a hostile hashrate majority. Finally, DropKick assumes that a post-quantum destination and signature already exist: Alex asks whether commit-reveal therefore protects only users who procrastinate, not a system that procrastinates, and conduition answers that yes, it is a prerequisite.

None of these variants has a BIP number or merged code. BIP-0361 places these protocols among the rescue protocols that can, in its view, be more efficient than zk-STARK proofs, at the price of a more delicate multi-step security model, without naming a variant. See quantum-safe rescue protocol, post-quantum migration, and Hourglass for P2PK.

*Bitcoin Optech Newsletter #348 states that Ruffing's scheme is generally known as the Guy Fawkes signature. In cryptography, a commitment followed by a reveal is a more general technique; this entry describes only its use as a post-quantum rescue protocol for Bitcoin.*
