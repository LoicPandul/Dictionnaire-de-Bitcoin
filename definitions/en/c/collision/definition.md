Situation in which two distinct inputs produce the same output through a function, in practice a hash function. A hash function turns data of any size into a fixed-size hash, so collisions necessarily exist, and all that is asked of a cryptographic hash function is that finding one be infeasible. This property is called collision resistance.

The cost of the search depends on the size of the hash. An $n$-bit hash can take $2^n$ values, but, as with two people sharing a birthday in a group, the probability that a draw lands on a value already seen grows quickly: on the order of $2^{n/2}$ attempts are enough to find a collision, versus $2^n$ to find an input that produces a given hash (a preimage). A 256-bit hash therefore offers 128 bits of security against collisions, and a 160-bit hash only 80 bits.

Take Alice and Bob, who lock funds together with a script whose hash is 160 bits long. An attacker who can produce collisions at that level does not need to invert the hash, only to find two inputs that share the same hash. That takes on the order of $2^{80}$ computations, which is heavy but no longer out of reach: BIP-0141 notes that, at the end of 2015, mining had already computed $2^{84}$ hashes since the creation of Bitcoin. This is why P2WSH (BIP-0141) locks the output with a 32-byte hash rather than the 20-byte hash of P2SH (BIP-0016), and why BIP-0341 does not support wrapping a Taproot output in P2SH: 80 bits of collision security becomes a risk as soon as an output contains data from several parties. In Script, `OP_RIPEMD160`, `OP_SHA1`, and `OP_HASH160` produce 160-bit hashes, while `OP_SHA256` and `OP_HASH256` produce 256-bit hashes.

Real collisions are not just a hypothesis. On February 23, 2017, CWI Amsterdam and Google announced the first collision on `SHA1` (the SHAttered project), at a cost of about nine quintillion computations (on the order of $2^{63}$, less than the $2^{80}$ of a blind search, because the attack exploits weaknesses in `SHA1`), or 6,500 CPU-years and 110 GPU-years. In September 2013, Peter Todd had opened a bounty on BitcoinTalk, a script that released bitcoin to whoever supplied two different messages with the same `SHA1` hash: it was claimed in February 2017 by a third party who, according to Todd, reused the published results. A quantum computer does not remove the threat: the algorithm of Brassard, Høyer, and Tapp finds collisions in fewer evaluations than a classical algorithm, but at the cost of a quantum memory of the same order (see GROVER'S ALGORITHM).

The protocol also protects against collisions between contexts. The tagged hashes of BIP-0340 prefix each piece of data with two copies of the `SHA256` of a tag specific to its use, so that a hash computed in one context cannot in practice be reinterpreted in another.

A collision can also serve as a building block. In November 2024, Ethan Heilman, Victor Kolobov, Avihu Levy, and Andrew Poelstra published ColliderScript, which enforces covenants without a consensus change: an equivalence test based on `SHA1` and `RIPEMD160` collisions links ordinary Bitcoin Script, which can read the transaction's signature, to a "Small Script" capable of arbitrary computation. The authors estimate the cost of each spend at about $2^{86}$ hash calls and the cost of an attack on the covenant at about $2^{110}$ under a triple-collision assumption. Heilman presented the paper on the bitcoindev list on November 7, 2024, and puts the cost of a spend at about 33 hours of the network's work. Heilman specifies that this cost currently limits the use of the construction: a spend would probably require dedicated ASICs, and the authors note that no implementation of `SHA1` and `RIPEMD160` in Small Script exists yet.

*A collision should not be confused with a preimage, which consists of finding an input that produces a given hash and costs far more. The word also refers, in another sense, to two elements targeting the same slot of a hash table, as in a feeler connection.*
