Acronym for *Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm*. Stateless hash-based signature, standardized by NIST in FIPS 205, published on August 13, 2024. It relies only on the presumed difficulty of finding preimages of a hash function and related properties, and the standard presents it as designed to resist a large quantum computer. The signer has no state to keep between two signatures, at the cost of signatures of several kB (see HASH-BASED SIGNATURE).

SLH-DSA combines two schemes: FORS (forest of random subsets), a few-time signature, and a variant of XMSS (see XMSS), itself built on a variant of WOTS+ (see WOTS). These variants differ from those of RFC 8391. An SLH-DSA key pair theoretically represents a very large number of FORS key pairs, $2^{63}$ to $2^{68}$ depending on the parameter set, all derived from a single seed, each authenticated by a WOTS+ key at a leaf of a hypertree, that is, a tree of XMSS trees. Each tree in the bottom layer signs the FORS keys, each leaf of a tree in a layer signs the root of a tree in the layer below, and a single tree at the top gives the root of the public key.

Let's take the SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s parameter set, where the values are $n = 16$ bytes long. Alice draws 3 random 16-byte values: `SK.seed`, secret, which generates all the WOTS+ and FORS secrets, `SK.prf`, secret, which serves as the key of the pseudorandom function producing the randomness $R$ of the message hash, and `PK.seed`, public, which separates the key pairs. She computes the root of the top tree, `PK.root`. Her public key, `PK.seed` followed by `PK.root`, is 32 bytes, and her private key 64 bytes. The hypertree has 7 layers of trees of height 9, or 63 levels, hence the $2^{63}$ FORS keys.

To sign a message $M$, Alice derives randomness $R$ from `SK.prf`, from $M$, and from a fresh 16-byte value (“hedged” variant, the default), then hashes $R$, her public key, and $M$. One part of the digest designates, without her having to remember anything, a tree of the bottom layer and a leaf of that tree, hence a specific FORS key. The other part of the digest is signed by this FORS key: in each of its 14 trees of 4,096 leaves, the signature reveals a secret designated by 12 bits of the digest, with its authentication path. The resulting FORS public key is then signed by the WOTS+ key of the leaf, whose tree is itself signed by the tree above, up to the top. The signature consists of $R$ (16 bytes), the FORS signature (2,912 bytes), and 7 XMSS signatures (4,928 bytes), for a total of 7,856 bytes. Bob finds the leaf by hashing in turn, recomputes the FORS public key from the revealed secrets, climbs the 7 layers, and accepts if the last root equals `PK.root`. He only hashes.

The pseudorandom choice of the leaf replaces the counter of stateful schemes. A leaf can be reused, because the security of a FORS key degrades gradually with the number of signatures, whereas a WOTS+ key becomes forgeable as soon as it is used a second time. The hypertree is large enough that, with at most $2^{64}$ signatures per key, the probability that a FORS key is used too often remains negligible: NIST notes that producing $2^{64}$ signatures at 10 billion per second would take more than 58 years.

FIPS 205 approves 12 parameter sets: 3 security levels (NIST categories 1, 3, and 5, for values of 16, 24, and 32 bytes), 2 variants, “s” for relatively small signatures and “f” for relatively fast signing, and 2 hash functions, SHA2 or SHAKE. Public key and signature sizes are the same for SHA2 and SHAKE. They range from 32 and 7,856 bytes for SLH-DSA-128s to 32 and 17,088 bytes for 128f, and up to 64 and 49,856 bytes for 256f (29,792 bytes for 256s). The standard defines a “pure” version, where the message is signed directly with a context of at most 255 bytes, and a “pre-hash” version that signs a digest of the message. The signature can be randomized by a fresh value (“hedged” variant, the default) or deterministic.

On Bitcoin, SLH-DSA is one of the candidates under study, but it is not activated. On December 15, 2024, Matt Corallo proposed on bitcoindev an `OP_SPHINCS` opcode in a Taproot script path. A version of BIP-0360 dated March 18, 2025, then named P2QRH, listed SLH-DSA-SHAKE-128s among its 3 post-quantum algorithms; the current version (P2MR) no longer specifies any post-quantum signature and cites SLH-DSA and ML-DSA as examples to examine. In November 2025, Conduition published an implementation (slhvk) that signs in 11 ms with SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s, versus 94 ms with PQClean, according to his measurements. In January 2026, he showed that a parallelized verifier reaches 63,971 signatures per second, versus 42,879 for libsecp256k1 on a single thread, but 278,836 for libsecp256k1 on 32 threads, on his machine: Optech describes a verification that can rival Schnorr in common cases.

Size remains one of the main obstacles. To reduce signatures, Mikhail Kudinov and Jonas Nick (Blockstream Research) proposed in December 2025 to limit the number of signatures per key: from $2^{64}$ to $2^{30}$, the signature goes from about 7,856 to 3,888 bytes. They estimate that 200 years of Bitcoin blocks contain at most $2^{35}$ signatures. The SHRINCS project reuses the FIPS 205 algorithms with a nonstandard parameter set limited to $2^{40}$ signatures, whose signature is 5,776 bytes (5,777 with a prefix byte), 26% smaller than SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s. In August 2026, Antoine Riard estimated at about 90 times (5,777 bytes versus 64) the overhead on the fee-bumping reserves of off-chain protocols that would use the stateless signature, unless a discount applies to these fields, and Conduition put the weight gap at about 14.8 times for a transaction with two inputs and two outputs; Pieter Wuille judged that a new witness cost rule, which would weigh computation more and size less, would probably be necessary for hash-based signatures. Conduition argues for SHA2 rather than SHAKE for Bitcoin. NIST also published on April 13, 2026 a draft, SP 800-230, which adds 6 parameter sets limited to $2^{24}$ signatures per key for signing software and certificates.

SLH-DSA descends from SPHINCS, published on ePrint in October 2014 by Daniel J. Bernstein, Daira Hopwood, Andreas Hülsing, Tanja Lange, Ruben Niederhagen, Louiza Papachristodoulou, Michael Schneider, Peter Schwabe, and Zooko Wilcox-O'Hearn (Eurocrypt 2015), with signatures of 41 kB. SPHINCS+ replaces the HORST signature with FORS and brings the signature down to about 8 kB at NIST level 1. It was submitted to NIST on November 30, 2017, with the team led by Hülsing. On July 5, 2022, NIST selected it along with CRYSTALS-Dilithium and Falcon, noting that it is larger and slower but valuable as a backup, because it relies on hash functions rather than lattices. FIPS 205 was first published as a draft on August 24, 2023, then in its final version on August 13, 2024, with 12 of the 36 SPHINCS+ parameter sets retained.

*In work on Bitcoin, the scheme is also often called SPHINCS+, the name of the scheme from which FIPS 205 is derived, or simply SPHINCS, although the latter designates the original 2015 scheme.*
