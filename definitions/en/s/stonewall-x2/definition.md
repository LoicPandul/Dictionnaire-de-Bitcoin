Specific form of Bitcoin transaction aimed at increasing users' privacy when spending, through collaboration with a third party who is not involved in the payment. This method simulates a mini-coinjoin between two participants while making a payment to a third party. Stonewall x2 transactions were proposed by the Samourai Wallet application, which shut down on April 24, 2024.

How it works is relatively simple: the sender uses a UTXO they own to make the payment and asks a third party for help, who also contributes a UTXO of their own. The transaction ends up with four outputs: two of them of equal amounts, one sent to the payment recipient's address and the other to an address belonging to the collaborator. A third output is sent back to another address of the collaborator, letting them recover their initial amount (a neutral action for them, apart from mining fees), and a last output goes back to an address belonging to the sender, which is the change from the payment. Three different roles are thus defined in Stonewall x2 transactions:
* The sender, who makes the actual payment;
* The collaborator, who makes bitcoin available to improve the transaction's anonymity set, while recovering their funds at the end, minus their half of the mining fees;
* The recipient, who may be unaware of the specific nature of the transaction and simply expects a payment from the sender.

The Stonewall x2 structure adds a great deal of entropy to the transaction and muddies the trail for chain analysis. Seen from the outside, such a transaction can be read as a small coinjoin between two people. In reality, however, it is a payment. This method therefore creates uncertainty in chain analysis, and even points toward false leads. Even if an outside observer manages to identify the Stonewall x2 transaction pattern, they will not have all the information. They will not be able to determine which of the two UTXOs of equal amounts corresponds to the payment. Moreover, they will not be able to tell who made the payment. Finally, they will not be able to determine whether the two input UTXOs come from two different people or belong to a single person who merged them. This last point is due to the fact that classic Stonewall transactions follow exactly the same pattern as Stonewall x2 transactions. From the outside, and without additional information about the context, it is impossible to tell a Stonewall transaction from a Stonewall x2 transaction. Yet the former are not collaborative transactions, whereas the latter are. This adds even more doubt about the spend.
