Electronic chip resistant to physical attacks, designed to store sensitive data and perform cryptographic operations in an isolated environment. In general computing, secure elements are found in bank cards, biometric passports, SIM cards, and the Trusted Platform Module (TPM) chips of computers. They are designed to resist side-channel attacks, fault injection attacks, and physical reverse engineering attempts. Secure elements are generally certified under the international Common Criteria (CC) standard, with assurance levels ranging from EAL1 to EAL7.

In the context of Bitcoin, secure elements are used in hardware wallets to protect private keys from unauthorized access, even if the device is physically compromised. The private key is generated and stored inside the chip, and signing operations are performed internally without ever exposing the key outside the component. Most hardware wallet manufacturers build a secure element into their devices: Ledger (with ST chips from STMicroelectronics), Coldcard (with ATECC608 chips from Microchip), BitBox02, Trezor Safe (with the TROPIC01 chip), and others. Some devices, such as the Blockstream Jade, take an alternative approach known as a virtual secure element, where the key is protected by a distributed encryption scheme rather than by a dedicated physical chip.
