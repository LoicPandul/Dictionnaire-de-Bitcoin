Sum that the protocol and users pay to miners to produce blocks, that is, the block subsidy plus transaction fees, over a given period. The Bitcoin Core code does not contain the expression, and the sources consulted give no formal definition of it. The expression is used to estimate what it costs an adversary to compete with honest miners: this cost follows revenue, since computing power is paid for out of revenue.

The link with security comes from the white paper. A chain stays secure as long as honest nodes control more computing power than an attacker; section 6 provides for miners to be paid with new coins, plus fees, and then for their pay to rely on fees alone once a predetermined number of coins is in circulation. It adds that an attacker more powerful than all the honest nodes would find it more profitable to play by the rules, since they would collect more new coins than the rest of the network. The higher the revenue, the more expensive it is to assemble hardware and energy consumption that outdo it, and the more expensive the attack. What an attack can achieve is bounded, as section 11 recalls: the attacker cannot create value or take coins that never belonged to them, and can only take back money they have just spent.

The Bitcoin Core code sets the ceiling of this revenue for each block. The coinbase transaction cannot pay out more than the block's fees plus the subsidy, otherwise the block is rejected (`bad-cb-amount`). The subsidy starts at 50 BTC and is halved every 210,000 blocks; it has been 3.125 BTC since block 840,000. Let's take a numerical example: over the 144 blocks from 973,152 to 973,295, total rewards reach about 452.2 BTC, of which about 2.2 BTC are fees, close to 0.5% (data from mempool.space). In this sample, security therefore rests almost entirely on the subsidy.

This is why the security budget serves as an argument in debates about the end of the subsidy. Two problems that the expression sometimes mixes must be told apart: how much revenue is needed, and where it will come from. For the first, there is no accepted answer. In June 2024, on Delving Bitcoin, CubicEarth wrote that nobody knows what Bitcoin's appropriate security budget is, and that it would be inappropriate, in a thread about the block size limit, to manipulate other variables to aim for a specific security budget that nobody has stated or agreed on. For the second, the most discussed route is a change to issuance, which the following proposal illustrates.

A June 2026 proposal on Delving Bitcoin does so: show1225 suggests a residual issuance of 0.25 BTC per block from block 1,680,000 (around 2040), offset, if demand is there, by burning a share of fees in the manner of EIP-1559. In this thread, sipa judges it “a complete non-starter”, and ajtowns notes that the subsidy still seems to be rising in dollar terms and expects miners to optimize their fee revenue more once that is no longer the case. micah541 believes the main risk is the gradual consolidation of miners rather than a sudden attack.

Researchers are also measuring the trend. The Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance (CCAF) published a report on October 20, 2026 noting that fees made up 0.67% of miner revenue over the 90 days to October 4, 2026, that they stayed below 5% most of the time, and that fee revenue varies from one day to the next 11.7 times more than the price of bitcoin. The authors propose an indicator, the Security Budget Health Indicator, to track the evolution rather than debate it. They note that the cost of an attack follows miner revenue, and that theoretical attacks such as selfish mining have not been observed on the main network.

*The security budget is measured in revenue, not in computing power, and should not be confused with hashrate.*
