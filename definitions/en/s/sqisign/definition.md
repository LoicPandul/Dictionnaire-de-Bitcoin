Post-quantum digital signature scheme based on isogenies between supersingular elliptic curves, whose public key and signature, combined, are the smallest of all the post-quantum signatures known to its authors. In its 2023 version, cited by the Chaincode Labs report, a signature weighs 177 bytes for a public key of 64 bytes, barely 3 times a Schnorr signature. In return, signing and verifying are markedly slower than with ML-DSA and Falcon, and the signature is difficult to implement. BIP-0360 dropped it in January 2025 for its performance, and it is among the 9 third-round candidates of the NIST additional signatures process, announced on May 14, 2026.

SQIsign stays in the world of elliptic curves, but it does not use the same hard problem as Schnorr. Two elliptic curves can be linked by maps called isogenies, which carry the points of one onto the other. The basic problem of this family is the following: given 2 curves, find an isogeny that goes from one to the other. It is related to a second problem: knowing the endomorphism ring of a curve, that is, the set of isogenies that map it back onto itself. For so-called supersingular curves, these 2 problems are equivalent: whoever knows the endomorphism rings of 2 curves can compute an isogeny between them. No algorithm, classical or quantum, is known to solve the endomorphism ring problem efficiently, and the security of SQIsign rests on it. Shor's algorithm, which breaks the discrete logarithm, does not apply to it.

The signature is a proof of knowledge of this secret, made non-interactive like a Schnorr signature. Let's take an example. Alice's public key is a curve $E_{pk}$, and her private key is the endomorphism ring of that curve. To sign, Alice randomly chooses a curve $E_{com}$ whose endomorphism ring she also knows: this is her commitment. The challenge, drawn from a hash of the public key, of $E_{com}$, and of the message, designates an isogeny that leads from $E_{pk}$ to a curve $E_{chl}$. Thanks to her secret, Alice can compute an isogeny that links $E_{chl}$ to $E_{com}$, and this is her response. Bob recomputes $E_{chl}$ from the challenge, verifies that the response is indeed an isogeny that starts from it, then recomputes the hash with the response's destination curve and verifies that it gives the challenge back. Alice's computation is the heavy part: it manipulates a variety of mathematical objects (quaternions, ideals, isogenies in dimension 2). The name of the scheme, Short Quaternion and Isogeny Signature, comes from the 2020 paper and cites two of its ingredients, quaternions and isogenies. This sketch follows version 3.0 of the specification.

Sizes remain tiny at every security level. Version 1.0 of the specification (June 2023) announces, at NIST level I, a public key of 64 bytes and a signature of 177 bytes. Version 2.0 (February 2025) brings them down to 65 and 148 bytes; version 3.0 (September 2026) raises the parameters after the discovery of a new attack on the endomorphism ring problem, and also changes the protocol to simplify the computation of the response; it brings them back up to 83 and 200 bytes. Its authors write that these are about 5 times smaller than those of Falcon and 12 times smaller than those of ML-DSA, counting key and signature together. An SLH-DSA-128s signature, by comparison, weighs 7,856 bytes.

The computational cost has dropped sharply, but it remains high. The Chaincode Labs report (May 2025), whose table covers SQIsign as submitted in 2023, estimates that signing with SQIsign costs about 135,000 times more than with ECDSA, and verifying about 830 times more. Version 3.0 announces, at level I and on an Intel Core i7-13700K processor, about 27.6 ms to sign and 3.6 ms to verify, durations that its authors consider to be of the same order as those of many other post-quantum candidates, while still being much slower than ML-DSA and Falcon. They acknowledge that the signature remains complex to implement, in particular to resist side-channel attacks: their own implementations do not yet run entirely in constant time. For Bitcoin, verification matters more, since every node verifies the signatures of each block it validates.

The isogeny family also carries a mistrust inherited from SIKE. This isogeny-based key encapsulation mechanism, a fourth-round candidate in the NIST standardization, was broken in 2022 by Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru, in a few dozen minutes at most on a single desktop computer core (about an hour according to the Chaincode report, which sees in it a source of doubt about the whole family). The authors of SQIsign point out that the 2022 and 2023 attacks on SIDH, of which SIKE is an instantiation, exploited an easier variant of isogeny problems, and that they do not affect the endomorphism ring problem.

On Bitcoin, SQIsign was considered and then set aside. Until January 2025, BIP-0360 planned to add it once approved, noting that Falcon signatures are about 4 times larger. Its version 0.6.0, of January 20, 2025, removes it “due to significant performance concerns”, estimating its performance at about 100,000 times worse than that of elliptic curve cryptography, which would be prohibitive for a blockchain. In July 2025, the proposal recalls that it offers the smallest signatures but the worst verification and the most complex implementation, and does not rule out coming back to it in light of the gains in its version 2.0. In January 2026, on the bitcoindev mailing list, Conduition recommends it to anyone seeking the smallest signatures, while judging it still immature, and Mikhail Kudinov estimates that it still needs time before being considered.

SQISign was presented in 2020 by Luca De Feo, David Kohel, Antonin Leroux, Christophe Petit, and Benjamin Wesolowski at the Asiacrypt conference. An expanded team submitted it to the NIST call for additional post-quantum signatures, which closed on June 1, 2023 and seeks schemes that rest on assumptions other than those of structured lattices, or short signatures with fast verification. Selected in the second round among 14 candidates, SQIsign moves on to the third round announced on May 14, 2026, where it is the only isogeny-based candidate.

*The name is written SQISign in the 2020 paper and SQIsign in the specifications submitted to NIST. The sizes given for SQIsign vary with the version of the specification each source refers to.*
