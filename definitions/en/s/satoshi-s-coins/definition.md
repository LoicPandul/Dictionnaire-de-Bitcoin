Coins mined in the very first blocks by a miner whom Sergio Lerner's analysis associates with Satoshi Nakamoto, and almost all of which, according to that analysis, have never been spent. They sit in P2PK outputs, whose public key is visible on the chain, which exposes the ones that have not moved to a future quantum computer. Their fate, whether to let a CRQC take them, slow down their spending, or make them unspendable, has become a frequently cited example in discussions of vulnerable coins.

The estimate comes from Sergio Demian Lerner. On April 17, 2013, he published on his blog Bitslog an analysis of the chain, blocks 0 to 36,288, that tracks the `ExtraNonce` fields of the coinbase transaction. Each point on the graph corresponds to 50 bitcoin created, and one set of blocks stands out: it comes almost entirely from a single entity that has been mining since block 1 and that has not, he writes, spent any coins, as far as one can tell by eye. Lerner states that he cannot guarantee 100% that all the points belong to Satoshi and estimates his fortune at about 1 million bitcoin. On April 24, 2013, he refines his count to 19,600 blocks, or 980,000 bitcoin, with a margin of error of about 0.1% at most, and speaks of “Satoshi (or the entity I associated with him).” In June 2020, he recalls having named this miner Patoshi, whom some identify as Satoshi Nakamoto, and who would have mined about 1.1 million bitcoin in 2009 and 2010. In August 2020, he describes the pattern that distinguishes this miner, a sweep of only part of the nonce space, and acknowledges that the link to Satoshi rests on unsigned emails, not on mathematical proof: it will never be certain. Chaincode's report (May 2025), for its part, gives a range of 600,000 to 1.1 million bitcoin for the Satoshi era, which it ties to a 2017 Fortune article, which itself cites only about 1 million bitcoin (a Chainalysis figure). These figures are estimates, not a verified list of addresses.

These coins sit in P2PK outputs. In Bitcoin v0.1.5, the miner code builds the coinbase output as a public key followed by `OP_CHECKSIG`. Block 1, dated January 9, 2009, also contains a 50-bitcoin output locked by a 65-byte public key, and it was still unspent on October 26, 2026, according to the Blockstream API. Not every coinbase of that era stayed in place: the one from block 9, also P2PK, was spent in block 170. BIP-0360 lists P2PK outputs, along with reused addresses and P2TR, among those that are fundamentally vulnerable to a long exposure attack, and expressly cites “Satoshi's coins” as an example. According to Chaincode's report, which reuses a 2023 query by Mark Erhardt, P2PK outputs account for about 0.025% of UTXOs and about 1,720,747 bitcoin, or 8.68% of the value of Bitcoin. This total also includes P2PK outputs other than Lerner's, and none of the sources consulted quantifies the overlap.

The debate these coins illustrate is summarized by BIP-0361, which distinguishes three responses: letting anyone take the vulnerable coins, which benefits whoever first has a quantum computer; allowing a slowed-down theft, which leads to transaction replacement battles with miners subsidized by the recovered coins; letting no one take them. The second path resembles Hourglass, the third QRAMP and phase B of BIP-0361. Cruz writes in the QRAMP thread that lost coins, including Satoshi's if he is no longer alive, would remain vulnerable without this measure. BIP-0361 judges that no quantum-safe rescue protocol can be built for P2PK, for lack of knowledge that only the rightful owner would have, and supports a Hourglass-type rule for this case. The Hourglass text itself cites Satoshi's coins at the top of its motivation, specifying that the problem is not limited to them.

Jameson Lopp, who argued in 2025 for burning vulnerable funds, does not see these coins as the obvious target: in *Against Allowing Quantum Recovery of Bitcoin* (March 16, 2025), he writes that the narrative in which they would be picked up by a quantum “miner” is “not so simple, unfortunately.” An attacker would first go after richer wallets whose address has been reused; he gives as an example an address holding 31,000 bitcoin, and asks why target 50 bitcoin at a time when the same effort would yield 250,000. He adds that he does not wish to reward actors capable of quantum computation by inflating the money supply. A sentence by Satoshi comes up again and again in this debate: on June 21, 2010, in the “Dying bitcoins” thread on the Bitcointalk forum, he writes: “Lost coins only make everyone else's coins worth slightly more.” Lopp and BIP-0361 cite it and add the same corollary: a coin recovered by a quantum computer lowers the value of everyone else's, like a theft committed against everyone.

No consensus rule settles this fate today. The sources do not say either who holds these keys or whether they are lost: Chaincode's report notes that coins from this era have not moved since they were mined in 2009 and 2010, which leads many observers to think they never will, and that the keys of 145 addresses dormant since 2009 were used to sign messages during the Kleiman v. Wright trial.
