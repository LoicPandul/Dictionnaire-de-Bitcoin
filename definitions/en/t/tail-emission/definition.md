Monetary policy mechanism that keeps a fixed block reward in place indefinitely after the initial issuance schedule of a cryptocurrency has run out. Unlike Bitcoin's model, where the block subsidy shrinks through halvings until it reaches zero, a tail emission guarantees a constant flow of newly created units with every block. Although highly controversial, it is a proposal that occasionally resurfaces in debates around Bitcoin.

Monero is the most notable example of this approach: since June 2022, every block has produced a fixed reward of 0.6 XMR, after the main issuance of about 18.1 million units ran out.

Proponents of tail emission argue that it solves the problem of funding network security in the long term, by guaranteeing a permanent incentive for miners without depending exclusively on transaction fees. They also argue that the inevitable loss of coins (forgotten keys, deaths of holders) would naturally offset the money creation, making the circulating supply converge toward a stable equilibrium. Opponents consider that it contradicts the principle of a fixed and deterministic maximum supply, a fundamental characteristic of Bitcoin. They also argue that permanent inflation dilutes the value of existing units, and that a sufficiently developed transaction fee market can secure the network on its own in the long term.

In the context of Bitcoin, adding a tail emission would require a hard fork and is a highly controversial proposal. Bitcoin's fixed monetary policy, with a cap of 21 million bitcoin, is regarded by a large part of the community as a fundamental attribute of the protocol.
