Economic principle that is the inverse of Gresham's law, stating that "good money drives out bad". Formalized in 1989 by the economist Peter Bernholz, with reference to the writings of the French historian Adolphe Thiers on the hyperinflation of the revolutionary assignats, this law describes a spontaneous market mechanism. When a currency deteriorates to the point of losing the confidence of economic agents, they end up refusing it in favor of a currency perceived as more reliable, despite legal constraints.

Thiers' law is observed mainly in situations of hyperinflation. When a national currency collapses, the laws imposing its legal tender status become ineffective: merchants demand to be paid in foreign currencies or in tangible goods. Historical examples include Zimbabwe, where the US dollar replaced the Zimbabwean dollar when the country dollarized in 2009, and Weimar Germany, where the mark collapsed in 1923 to the point that trade was conducted in foreign currencies or by barter.

Some bitcoiners invoke Thiers' law to argue that Bitcoin could gradually establish itself as a medium of exchange in economies where the national currency is heavily depreciated, going beyond its role as a store of value.
