Mechanism that associates a precise point in time with an event, a piece of data, or a message. In computing generally, a timestamp is used to determine the chronological order of operations and to verify data integrity over time.

On Bitcoin, timestamps establish the chronology of transactions and blocks. Each block in the blockchain contains a timestamp indicating the approximate time of its creation. Satoshi Nakamoto even speaks of a “timestamp server” in the Bitcoin white paper to describe what would be called the “blockchain” today. The role of timestamping on Bitcoin is to determine the chronology of transactions, so that it is possible to determine, without any central authority, which transaction came first. This mechanism lets every user verify the absence of an earlier transaction, and so prevents a malicious user from double spending. Satoshi Nakamoto justifies this mechanism in the white paper with the famous sentence: “The only way to confirm the absence of a transaction is to be aware of all transactions.” The standard is based on Unix time, which counts the total number of seconds elapsed since January 1, 1970.

*Block timestamps are relatively flexible on Bitcoin: for a timestamp to be valid, it only needs to be greater than the median time past (MTP) of the 11 blocks preceding it and less than the node's local time plus 2 hours. Up to Bitcoin Core 26, this upper bound relied on the median of the times reported by peers (network-adjusted time), which was removed from consensus in Bitcoin Core 27.0.*
