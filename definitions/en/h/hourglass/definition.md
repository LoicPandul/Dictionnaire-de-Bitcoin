Soft fork proposal that would limit the speed at which P2PK outputs can be spent, so that a quantum computer able to recover their private keys could not drain them within a few hours. It freezes no coins: it spreads out the return of old bitcoin to circulation, over several months in the first version and over more than 32 years in the second, whether it is their legitimate owners or a quantum attacker who moves them. Hunter Beast and Michael Casey wrote it, and the text has not been assigned a BIP number.

A P2PK output, the format of Bitcoin's early years, contains the public key directly in its script. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could derive the private key from it without anyone spending anything, so these outputs are durably exposed, as described in the long exposure attack entry. According to the text of the proposal, about 1.7 million bitcoin sit in about 34,000 P2PK addresses, with an average balance of 50 bitcoin. With no limit at all, more than 6,000 P2PK transactions would fit in a block, and those 1.7 million bitcoin could be spent in barely more than an hour once the keys are recovered (the text says "a few hours"). The authors reject two responses: burning the P2PK coins on a fixed date, which would reduce the supply below 21 million and could be seen as confiscatory, or doing nothing, which would cause a supply shock on the market.

The first version, "Hourglass spending rules", is dated April 29, 2025, and its announcement on bitcoindev dates from the end of that month. It sets two rules: only one P2PK output can be spent per block, and no new P2PK output can be created. This amounts to at most about 7,200 bitcoin per day, according to the text. The acknowledgments credit the original idea to Charlie Glahe.

On February 10, 2026, a second version was announced on bitcoindev, after feedback from economic actors who judged the first one insufficient. Only one P2PK output can still be spent per block, and the transaction can no longer take more than 1 bitcoin from it: if the spent output is worth more, the transaction must send the rest back to the same script, and no P2PK output can be created to another address. Let's take an example. Alice owns a P2PK output of 50 bitcoin: she can send 1 bitcoin elsewhere in a block, with the rest of the output returning to the same script, so it takes at least 50 blocks to empty it completely. For all P2PK outputs together, this comes to about 144 bitcoin per day, or more than 32 years to move everything, against 450 bitcoin per day of block subsidy according to the text. The authors consider it possible that such a limit would create a fee market, in which those who want to move P2PK outputs would bid for the single slot available in each block, to the benefit of miners. Bitcoin Optech newsletter #395 (March 6, 2026) notes that these values are somewhat arbitrary, but that respondents found them a reasonable focal point.

Neither version specifies an activation mechanism or a timeline, and neither proposes a reference implementation. Both texts live in a personal fork of the BIPs repository (cryptoquick/bips), with no request to merge them into the official repository, and their branch has not moved since their single commit, according to the repository as consulted on October 26, 2026.

The proposal drew sharp objections. On the first version, Jameson Lopp sees it, at first glance, as a half-measure in May 2025, and Francis Pouliot raises a "Concept NACK" on May 3, 2025: in his view, 1.7 million bitcoin may represent roughly one week of global trading volume, and trying to manage the price through spending restrictions strikes him as a terrible idea. On the second version, several participants dispute the principle itself. Optech sums up the objection this way: possession of the secret keys is the only way the protocol identifies ownership, and even in the event of a cryptographic break, it should not add restrictions on the ownership or movement of coins. On March 3, 2026, Brandon Black answers this type of proposal with a firm and open-ended "no", and accepts disabling old signatures only in the event of an immediate and total break of cryptography. The Chaincode report (May 2025), for its part, relays the critics' argument: the proposal would set a precedent that, under certain conditions, the network would allow transfers without the legitimate authorization of the original owner. Jameson Lopp prefers the second version to the first, while finding the 1 bitcoin threshold fairly arbitrary.

Hourglass is one of the responses to the fate of vulnerable coins, alongside QRAMP and BIP-0361 (see POST-QUANTUM MIGRATION). BIP-0361 declares itself compatible with an Hourglass-style BIP for spending P2PK outputs, provided it is activated no later than the activation of its phase B, and its authors support it: no one currently knows how to build a quantum-safe rescue protocol for P2PK, for lack of knowledge that the legitimate owner would have and an attacker would not. BIP-0360 (P2MR) also refers to Hourglass for the supply shock, which it considers outside its scope. See also Satoshi's coins, whose outputs are in P2PK format.
