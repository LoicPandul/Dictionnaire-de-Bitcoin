Family of digital signature schemes built solely from a hash function, without a mathematical problem such as the discrete logarithm. Their security rests only on the properties of this function, in particular the difficulty of finding a preimage or a second preimage, which puts them out of reach of Shor's algorithm and makes them one of the families of post-quantum cryptography. On Bitcoin, they account for a large share of recent work on post-quantum signatures, even though lattice-based cryptography is also discussed there.

Most rely on the same building block, the one-time signature. The private key is a series of secret numbers, the public key is the list of their hashes, and signing amounts to revealing some of these secrets, chosen according to the hash of the message. The verifier hashes the received secrets and checks that the result matches the public key. This idea comes from Leslie Lamport, in the mid-1970s (see LAMPORT SIGNATURE). It has two flaws: a key must sign only one message, because each signature reveals part of the secrets, and the signature is large, 8,192 bytes with `SHA256`. The Winternitz signature (see WOTS) shortens the signature by hashing each secret in a chain, which makes it possible to sign several bits with a single value.

To sign several messages with a single public key, Ralph Merkle proposed in 1979, in his Stanford thesis, to arrange many one-time keys under a binary tree. Each leaf holds a one-time key, each node is the hash of its 2 children, and the public key essentially boils down to the root of this Merkle tree. To sign, Alice takes a leaf she has never used, signs the message with that leaf's key, and attaches the neighboring nodes along the path from the leaf to the root. With a Winternitz signature, as in XMSS and LMS, Bob recomputes the leaf key from the signature, climbs the tree with these nodes, and compares the result with the root he knows. A tree of height 10 thus holds 1,024 one-time keys under a 32-byte root with `SHA256`.

Depending on how they handle one-time use, hash-based signatures fall into 4 categories:

* one-time schemes (Lamport, Winternitz), where a key must sign only one message: a second signature weakens security (slowly for Lamport, very quickly for Winternitz) and enables forgeries;
* few-time schemes, such as HORS, proposed by Leonid and Natan Reyzin in 2002, or FORS, whose security degrades gradually with each signature, whereas that of a Winternitz key collapses with the second;
* stateful schemes, such as XMSS and LMS, which gather one-time keys under a Merkle tree and require the signer to remember which leaves it has already used;
* stateless schemes, such as SLH-DSA, which stack trees up to several tens, even hundreds, of billions of billions of leaves ($2^{63}$ to $2^{68}$) and choose the leaf pseudorandomly from the message, so the signer has nothing to remember.

State is the main practical difficulty of stateful schemes. With XMSS or LMS, the private key changes with each signature, and if the same leaf signs 2 different messages, RFC 8391 (XMSS) and RFC 8554 (LMS) warn that no security guarantee remains. NIST therefore approves these schemes, in its SP 800-208 recommendation of October 2020, only in hardware modules that never export the private key, even encrypted, and judges that they are not suitable for general use. It intends them mainly for cases such as signing firmware updates. For a Bitcoin wallet, Mikhail Kudinov and Jonas Nick (Blockstream Research) pointed out in December 2025 that restoring a stale backup would cause already-used leaves to be reused, and therefore enable forgeries. Stateless schemes avoid this trap, at the price of much larger signatures.

This size difference shows in the numbers. According to the table in the Chaincode report (Anthony Milton and Clara Shikhelman, May 2025), a Schnorr signature is 64 bytes for a 32-byte public key. An XMSS-SHA2_10_256 signature is 2,500 bytes according to RFC 8391, for a 68-byte public key, and an SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s signature is 7,856 bytes according to FIPS 205, for a 32-byte public key. Public keys stay small, since they boil down to a Merkle root and a public seed, but the signatures of these two schemes are thousands of bytes long. Lattice-based signatures, such as ML-DSA (2,420 bytes for the smallest variant, the same order of magnitude as XMSS) or Falcon-512 (666 bytes), are more compact than those of SLH-DSA (see LATTICE-BASED CRYPTOGRAPHY).

This cost comes with more conservative assumptions. Any signature of a message of arbitrary size already hashes that message: a hash-based signature adds no mathematical problem and requires only properties of the hash function (preimage resistance, pseudorandomness), whereas lattice-based or isogeny-based families also rely on the hardness of a mathematical problem. Against a quantum computer, the generic attack that matters against these schemes is Grover's algorithm, which speeds up the search for a preimage only quadratically: RFC 8391 concludes that doubling the size of the hashes is enough to keep the targeted security level. The Chaincode report also notes that these methods are the ones that have received the most scrutiny of all post-quantum approaches.

On Bitcoin, the argument carries even more weight because the security of the protocol already depends on `SHA256`, applied twice to identify blocks and transactions. On May 20, 2026, on the bitcoindev mailing list, Conduition summarized the appeal of this family: assumptions strictly weaker than those Bitcoin already depends on, a simple implementation, fast verification, and a long-term backup role. Verifying such a signature boils down to hash computations and comparisons. BIP-0347 notes that with `OP_CAT`, a Lamport signature can be verified in Script by hashing and concatenating stack elements, and Conduition published a prototype of this kind for a Winternitz signature in 2025.

Several Bitcoin-related works date from 2025 and 2026. In their paper *Hash-based Signature Schemes for Bitcoin* (ePrint 2025/2203, December 2025), Kudinov and Nick review existing constructions and show that by limiting the number of signatures per key to $2^{40}$, $2^{30}$, or $2^{20}$ instead of $2^{64}$, and with variants such as WOTS+C, FORS+C, or PORS+FP, a stateless signature drops from about 7,856 to 4,000 bytes ($2^{40}$), 3,500 bytes ($2^{30}$), or 3,000 bytes ($2^{20}$). They estimate that, without an increase in block size, 200 years of Bitcoin blocks hold at most $2^{35}$ signatures of about 3 kB, and state that their parameter sets illustrate trade-offs without constituting a proposal. They also estimate, by counting calls to the `SHA256` compression function, that the verification cost per byte of their parameter sets remains of the same order as Schnorr's, or lower. Conduition made the SLH-DSA signature markedly faster in late 2025, then showed in January 2026 that the verification throughput of his parallelized implementation equals that of Schnorr on a single thread, without reaching that of Schnorr on several threads. SHRINCS, whose BIP draft appeared in August 2026, combines a stateful signature of 548 bytes at minimum and a stateless fallback that reuses the SLH-DSA algorithms with a nonstandard parameter set.

These schemes have limits of their own. Their public key is most often a Merkle root, sometimes accompanied by a public seed, without algebraic structure: Kudinov and Nick find no equivalent of BIP-0032's non-hardened derivation, and their multisignature and threshold signature constructions remain interactive or costly, far from the efficiency of MuSig2. Their size also raises the question of fees: in August 2026, Pieter Wuille argued that after Q-day, these signatures would probably need a new witness cost rule, which would weight computation more heavily than size.

As of October 25, 2026, no hash-based signature is activated on Bitcoin. Bitcoin Core contains no implementation of one, BIP-0360 (P2MR) specifies no post-quantum signature and cites SLH-DSA and ML-DSA as candidates to examine, and the SHRINCS draft defines only the scheme, its deployment being a matter for a separate proposal. On the standards side, NIST approved LMS and XMSS, with conditions, in recommendation SP 800-208 (October 2020), then standardized SLH-DSA in FIPS 205 (August 13, 2024).

*Schemes in this family are mainly distinguished by how they handle one-time use: one-time or few-time building blocks, assembled into stateful or stateless schemes.*
