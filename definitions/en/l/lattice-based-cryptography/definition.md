Family of post-quantum cryptography whose security rests on problems believed to be hard in Euclidean lattices, regular grids of points in a high-dimensional space. It provides digital signatures (ML-DSA, and FN-DSA, which is being standardized) and a key establishment mechanism (ML-KEM), with keys and signatures on the order of a kilobyte. No verified quantum algorithm solves these problems efficiently to date, whereas Shor's algorithm breaks the discrete logarithm on which ECDSA and Schnorr rely.

A Euclidean lattice is the set of points obtained by adding up a few basis vectors with integer coefficients. In 2 dimensions, it forms an infinite grid of points, and the sum of 2 points of the grid is still a point of the grid. Seemingly simple questions can be asked about it: what is the shortest distance between 2 points of the lattice? Which lattice point is closest to a randomly chosen point? In a well-chosen high-dimensional lattice, and without knowing how it was built, no one knows how to answer them efficiently, with either a classical computer or a quantum computer.

The schemes used in practice do not manipulate these grids directly, but 2 problems that reduce to them. The first, LWE (Learning With Errors), asks to recover a secret from linear equations each distorted by a small error. Without the error, Gaussian elimination would suffice. With the error, each combination of equations amplifies the noise, and the best known algorithms take exponential time. The second problem, SIS (Short Integer Solution), asks to find a nonzero solution with small coefficients to a linear system $A \cdot t = 0$. Regev linked LWE to approximation problems on lattices, long studied and considered in their worst case: an algorithm that broke LWE, for well-chosen parameters, would make it possible to solve them, with a quantum computer if need be, which gives LWE security guarantees that most other cryptographic constructions lack.

In their raw form, these problems give enormous keys: their size grows as the square of the dimension. To reduce them, modern schemes add an algebraic structure, an idea from the NTRU cryptosystem. Vectors become polynomials, which reduces the size of keys and allows fast computation through a discrete Fourier transform over the integers modulo q (NTT). ML-KEM and ML-DSA thus work on modules of polynomials with 256 coefficients, hence the prefix ML (Module-Lattice); ML-KEM relies on the MLWE variant of LWE, and ML-DSA on MLWE and on a module variant of SIS (MSIS); FN-DSA works on NTRU lattices and relies on SIS.

ML-KEM (FIPS 203) establishes a shared secret between 2 parties, and serves as a post-quantum alternative to ECDH-type key exchange. ML-DSA (FIPS 204) signs according to what Blockstream calls “Schnorr with vectors”: NIST describes it as a Schnorr-type signature, in which the signer commits to a random value, derives a challenge from the hash of this commitment and the message, then responds with a combination of their secret and this random value, with matrices and vectors replacing Schnorr's numbers. FN-DSA, derived from Falcon, follows another construction, more compact but trickier to implement.

Compared with hash-based signatures, lattices offer a different tradeoff. An ML-DSA-44 signature is 2,420 bytes for a public key of 1,312 bytes, and a Falcon-512 signature is 666 bytes for a key of 897 bytes, whereas SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+ 128s) produces a signature of 7,856 bytes with a key of 32 bytes. The Chaincode Labs report (May 2025) judges this tradeoff more balanced for lattices. Lattices also keep an algebraic structure close to that of elliptic curves: with a public matrix $A$ and 2 secrets $x$ and $y$, $A(x + y) = Ax + Ay$, like $(x + y)G = xG + yG$. In May 2026, Blockstream sees in this a path toward post-quantum multisignatures or aggregations, provided the secrets are kept short, since their size grows when they are combined.

In return, these schemes rely on assumptions less battle-tested than those of SLH-DSA, which only requires the resistance of a hash function, and implementing them demands care. FN-DSA signing relies on floating-point arithmetic, which must run in constant time, something hard to guarantee on all processors; without an FPU, it is emulated with integers. The website of Hawk, the only lattice-based scheme among the 9 candidates for the third round of NIST's additional signatures process (announced on May 14, 2026), states that the scheme is not secure; it was withdrawn from the process on July 29, 2026.

On Bitcoin, the question remains open. BIP-0360, which specifies no post-quantum signature, cites ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as examples to examine before any use. On May 21, 2026, on Delving Bitcoin, Nikita Karetnikov asked why Blockstream had so far concentrated on hashing although its own comparison favors lattices; the message had been sent on May 19 to the bitcoindev list. Conduition replied there on May 20 that hash-based signatures remain attractive for their weaker assumptions, their simplicity, their fast verification, and their role as a long-term fallback. For Lightning, Olaoluwa Osuntokun estimated in May 2026 that no post-quantum scheme alone fills all the functions of an elliptic-curve key: ML-KEM would encrypt connections, ML-DSA would sign off-chain, and SLH-DSA on-chain. The PQLN paper (September 2026) proposes a hybrid extension of Lightning based on ML-DSA and ML-KEM.

The foundations of this family are old. In 1996, Ajtai built the first cryptographic primitive whose security rests on the hardness of lattice problems in their worst case. Hoffstein, Pipher, and Silverman published the NTRU cryptosystem in 1998, and Oded Regev introduced LWE in 2005. NIST launched its post-quantum standardization in December 2016, selected 4 algorithms in July 2022, 3 of them based on structured lattices (Kyber, Dilithium, and Falcon) and one hash-based (SPHINCS+), then published ML-KEM and ML-DSA on August 13, 2024; FN-DSA is to follow in FIPS 206, still in preparation in October 2026.
