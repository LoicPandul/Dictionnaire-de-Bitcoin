Quantum attack against a public key that stays visible for a long time, longer than a transaction waits in the mempool. The attacker uses a CRQC to recover the private key from that public key, then signs a transaction that sends the funds to an address the attacker controls. As long as the funds remain in the output, the attacker has all the time needed.

Let's take an example. In 2010, Alice received 50 BTC on a P2PK output, whose script contains her public key in plain view. An attacker equipped with a CRQC reads this key on the blockchain, but nothing forces the attacker to act right away: Shor's algorithm can run as long as needed, and the spend comes afterward. No transaction from Alice is circulating, so there is nothing to front-run. The only race left is against Alice herself, who can move her funds to an output that does not reveal a key, and against other holders of a CRQC.

The outputs exposed to this risk are those whose public key is visible on the blockchain: P2PK, P2MS, and P2TR as soon as the funds are received, and any output whose address was reused after a spend that revealed a public key. A circulated xpub creates a similar risk without being visible on the blockchain: it allows a CRQC that obtains it to recover the non-hardened private keys derived from it. For details on these cases, see exposed public key. According to BIP-0361, more than 34% of bitcoin had a public key already revealed on the blockchain as of March 1, 2026; the dormant coins from Satoshi's era, in P2PK, are part of this, and the May 2025 Chaincode Labs report relays for them a 2017 estimate of 600,000 to 1.1 million BTC.

BIP-0360 considers these attacks probably the first that Bitcoin would suffer, because they do not require a particularly fast quantum computer: each key can be broken slowly. Short exposure attacks, which must succeed while the transaction waits for confirmation, require a faster machine. BIP-0360 concludes that preparing for long exposure is more urgent than preparing for short exposure. Its answer, P2MR, does not depend on post-quantum signatures: an output type with no key path does not reveal a public key until it is spent, provided its keys are not reused. P2TR could also be handled by a soft fork that would disable the key path, according to Chaincode.

For outputs that are already exposed, the situation is less straightforward. The owner can move the funds before the CRQC arrives, to an output type that hides the key. But dormant, lost, or forgotten coins will not move, and BIP-0361 notes that no known rescue protocol protects P2PK outputs, for lack of information that the owner would hold and the attacker would not. This is the core of the debate on post-quantum migration: burn these coins or let them be stolen, with or without an announced deadline.

The name dates from BIP-0360. Up to its version 0.5.1, these attacks were called *long range attacks*, as in the Chaincode Labs report; version 0.6.0 of January 20, 2025 chose "long exposure" to avoid confusion with the vocabulary of block reorganization attacks.

*Not to be confused with the short exposure attack, which targets a spend in progress.*
