American computer scientist specializing in distributed systems, 2013 Turing Award winner, at Microsoft Research since 2001. Two of his works touch on Bitcoin: a one-time signature scheme and the Byzantine Generals Problem.

Around 1975, at Berkeley, Whitfield Diffie described to him the problem of a digital signature for documents. Lamport immediately proposed a solution, which Diffie and Hellman mentioned in 1976 in “New Directions in Cryptography.” He described it in October 1979 in an SRI International technical report, “Constructing Digital Signatures from a One Way Function.” This is the origin of the Lamport signature, which relies only on a one-way function, in practice a hash function.

In July 1982, he published with Marshall Pease and Robert Shostak “The Byzantine Generals Problem” in *ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems*. Generals who are separated from one another, some of them traitors, must agree on a single decision by exchanging messages. Regarding the 1980 paper “Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults,” Lamport clarifies that SRI researchers on the SIFT project had formulated the problem before his arrival, although he had already uncovered Byzantine faults himself, that the solutions without signatures come from Shostak and Pease, and that his contribution is the solution with signatures.

On November 13, 2008, replying to James A. Donald on the cryptography mailing list, Satoshi Nakamoto wrote: “The proof-of-work chain is a solution to the Byzantine Generals' Problem.” Bitcoin's proof-of-work chain is thus an answer to this problem, although Satoshi does not cite Lamport and the white paper does not mention him.
