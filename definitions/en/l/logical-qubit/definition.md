Reliable qubit obtained by spreading a single unit of quantum information across several physical qubits, whose quantum error correction detects and corrects errors as the computation proceeds. The physical qubit is the hardware object (a trapped ion, a superconducting circuit); the logical qubit is the qubit the algorithm uses, and, when the physical qubits are below the error threshold of the code, it errs less often than each of the physical qubits that make it up. It is in this unit that the size of a quantum computer capable of breaking Bitcoin's cryptography (CRQC) is counted: on the order of 1,200 to 1,450 logical qubits for an elliptic curve defined over a 256-bit prime field, such as secp256k1, according to the estimate published in 2026 by Ryan Babbush and coauthors (Google Quantum AI), versus 2,330 according to the one by Martin Roetteler and coauthors (2017).

The logical qubit addresses a precision gap. In 2024, the best processors with many qubits only just reach 99.9% reliability on operations that entangle 2 qubits, or about one error per 1,000 operations, whereas many useful algorithms require fewer than one error per $10^{10}$ operations. Shor's algorithm, which chains tens of millions to billions of operations depending on the targeted key, therefore cannot run directly on physical qubits: the result would be wrong long before the end. Error correction has to bridge this gap by encoding each logical qubit on a group of physical qubits, so that an error on one of them is spotted and repaired without the encoded information being lost.

Google's Willow processor gives a concrete picture of what a logical qubit is. In the experiment published in December 2024, 101 of its 105 physical qubits serve a single logical qubit: 49 data qubits, arranged in a 7-by-7 grid, carry the information; 48 measure qubits, interleaved among them, monitor their neighbors; 4 additional qubits serve to remove leakage from the data qubits to unwanted energy levels. This distance-7 surface code follows the rule of $2d^2 - 1$ physical qubits for a distance $d$ (without margin between neighboring logical qubits), which gives 97 here, not counting the 4 leakage qubits. The resulting logical qubit errs in 0.143% of error-correction cycles, and it retains its information 2.4 times longer than the best of the physical qubits that make it up. This logical qubit is used as a memory: the experiment measures how long it retains a state, without using it in a computation.

The number of physical qubits per logical qubit is not fixed. It depends on the error rate of the physical qubits, the reliability the computation requires, and the code used. The Chaincode Labs report (May 2025) puts it on the order of 10 to 1,000 depending on the technology and the correction method. In 2012, Austin Fowler and coauthors indicated that a surface code requires at least 13 physical qubits per logical qubit, and that a logical qubit reliable enough for a real computation rather mobilizes on the order of 1,000 to 10,000. To factor a 2,000-bit number, with physical qubits ten times more reliable than the threshold of the code, they counted about 14,500 physical qubits per logical qubit, or 58 million physical qubits for the 4,000 computational logical qubits alone, with the entire machine requiring about one billion once the production of auxiliary states is included.

Recent estimates come down much lower. In May 2025, Craig Gidney (Google Quantum AI) calculated that a 2,048-bit RSA key could be factored in less than a week with fewer than 1,600 logical qubits, assuming physical qubits that err once in 1,000 operations and aiming for a logical error of $10^{-15}$ per qubit per cycle. Each logical qubit in the hot zone occupies a distance-25 surface code, that is $2 \times (25+1)^2 = 1\,352$ physical qubits, and the logical qubits in the cold zone, where they wait to be used, are packed more densely, at 430 physical qubits each. The whole fits in a little under 900,000 physical qubits, which he rounds to a million. In 2019, the same factoring still required 20 million noisy qubits according to Gidney and Martin Ekerå.

For Bitcoin's curve, the reference estimate was long the one by Roetteler, Michael Naehrig, Krysta Svore, and Kristin Lauter (2017), since lowered considerably. These authors showed that the discrete logarithm on an elliptic curve defined over an $n$-bit prime field can be computed with at most $9n + 2\lceil \log_2 n \rceil + 10$ logical qubits. For $n = 256$, this gives $2\,304 + 16 + 10 = 2\,330$ logical qubits, and their simulation on NIST's P-256 curve counts about 126 billion Toffoli gates, elementary 3-qubit operations. The bound on qubits holds for any curve of this size, hence for secp256k1. In March 2026, Ryan Babbush and coauthors (Google Quantum AI) announce for secp256k1 a circuit of at most 1,200 logical qubits and 90 million Toffoli gates, or at most 1,450 logical qubits and 70 million Toffoli gates: this is a trade-off between the number of qubits and the number of gates. They do not publish the circuits, only a zero-knowledge proof of their size; in June 2026, André Schrottenloher described public circuits of the same order, from 1,208 to 1,462 logical qubits. At comparable classical security, the elliptic curve requires fewer logical qubits than RSA: about 1,200 to 1,450 for Bitcoin's curve versus about 2,043 for a 3,072-bit RSA key according to Gidney (2025). In 2017, the gap was much larger: 2,330 versus 6,146. The corresponding number of physical qubits then depends on the quality of the machines, the error-correcting code, and the architecture chosen, which partly explains the gap between published estimates.

These orders of magnitude can be compared with manufacturers' roadmaps. According to Chaincode, a commercially useful quantum computer would require at least on the order of 1,000 logical qubits; IBM plans, from 2033, a machine (Blue Jay) of at most 2,000 logical qubits, and Intel partnered in February 2025 with Japan's AIST institute to aim for a system of tens of thousands of qubits, without specifying whether they would be logical, in the early 2030s. These are objectives announced by manufacturers and institutes, not results. BIP-0360 moreover describes a CRQC in this same unit, as a machine able to keep enough logical qubits coherent to run Shor's algorithm efficiently.

*A qubit count quoted without qualification usually refers to physical qubits, at least for existing machines: in the 2024 experiment, Willow devotes 101 of its 105 physical qubits to a single logical qubit.*
