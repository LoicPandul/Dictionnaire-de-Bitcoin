Weight calculation rule that counts each byte of a transaction's witness as 1 weight unit, against 4 for all other bytes. Introduced by SegWit (BIP-0141), it makes a signature byte weigh four times less on the limit of a block than an input or output byte, hence its name, a 75% discount.

The weight of a transaction equals 3 times its size without witness, plus its total size with witness, which amounts to counting 4 weight units (WU) per non-witness byte and 1 per witness byte. Consensus caps the weight of a block at 4,000,000 WU, and fees are calculated on the virtual size, that is, the weight divided by 4 and rounded up (see VBYTES and WEIGHT UNIT - WU). The discount therefore does not act on the rate: it reduces the number of weight units of the bytes it affects, and the per-vbyte rate then applies to all of them in the same way.

Let's take an example. Alice creates a transaction of 100 non-witness bytes and 1,000 witness bytes. Its weight is $100 \times 4 + 1{,}000 \times 1 = 1{,}400$ WU, or 350 vbytes. If those 1,000 bytes were outside the witness, it would weigh 4,400 WU, or 1,100 vbytes. For a block, the cap of 4,000,000 WU is equivalent to the old 1 MB limit when no transaction has a witness, and its actual size approaches 4 MB in the extreme case of a block made almost entirely of witness data.

Pieter Wuille justified the discount at Scaling Bitcoin, in Hong Kong, in December 2015: a signature does not enter the UTXO set and can be pruned, so the discount reduces the incentive to burden that set. For the factor of 4, he cited transaction validation, which he estimated to be about 7 times faster in Bitcoin Core 0.12 thanks to libsecp256k1. BIP-0141 adopts a single limit on weight rather than two separate limits (1 MB of base data, 3 MB of witness): two limits would make block optimization and fee estimation nearly impossible, and the witness would cost almost nothing once the first limit was reached. SegWit applies on mainnet from block 481,824, on August 24, 2017.

The discount is at the center of several debates. Tapscript, Taproot's script language, does not apply the 10,000-byte limit on script size (BIP-0342), which is now bounded only by the weight of the block. The Ordinals documentation states that the content of inscriptions is stored in Taproot scripts spent through the script path, which "receive the witness discount," making their storage relatively cheap (see INSCRIPTIONS). On December 27, 2023, GregTonoski opened a thread on [Delving Bitcoin](https://delvingbitcoin.org/t/bug-spammers-get-bitcoin-blockspace-at-discounted-price-lets-fix-it/327) titled "[BUG]: spammers get Bitcoin blockspace at discounted price. Let's fix it": in his view, a byte should cost the same price wherever it sits in the transaction, and a simple transaction pays more for its space than a complex one. Murch replied the next day that the parts of a transaction do not have the same footprint on the system. On December 30, moonsettler proposed a formula that would remove only the advantage of a disproportionate witness, while judging that BRC-20, which in his view makes up most of the spam, would be little affected by it. On January 8, 2024, ProofOfKeags wrote that the witness is discounted because, in certain situations during synchronization, it can be pruned at little risk, which cannot be done with data that touches the transaction graph. On January 9, Pieter Wuille objected that counting every byte of the current serialization equally is no less arbitrary than discounting part of it, and that the discount also limits the room a transaction can take from others. For the meaning of the word spam in these exchanges, see SPAM.

The topic returns with post-quantum signatures, which are larger than current signatures. The [Optech newsletter](https://bitcoinops.org/en/newsletters/2026/10/02/) of October 2, 2026, which summarizes Conduition's project to compress the signatures of a block into a single SNARK, notes that a discount large enough to make hash-based signatures (fast to verify but heavy) competitive in fees would increase archival storage by several TB per year. In the [August 7, 2026 newsletter](https://bitcoinops.org/en/newsletters/2026/08/07/), Anthony Towns argues that the capacity reserved for these signatures should remain unusable for ordinary data, and a draft BIP by MrHash proposes a prunable block region for arbitrary data, counted at the discount; Antoine Poinsot and Pieter Wuille respond that data nodes do not need to retain in order to accept a block is not part of Bitcoin's consensus in any meaningful sense.

*Not to be confused with SegWit, of which the discount is only one rule. The discount does not reduce the actual size of a transaction, only its weight.*
