Measure of trust computed from the relationships between accounts (who follows whom, who mutes whom), used to decide whom to trust or what to display without a central authority deciding. On Nostr, it is used to filter spam and rank accounts.

The term comes from cryptography: the PGP web of trust is a model in which one verifies that a public key really belongs to a person through the signatures of other users, rather than through a hierarchy of certificate authorities. Nostr applies the same name to social relationships, but the point is no longer to establish who owns a key: the graph is used to estimate the reliability of an account. A key is created there freely, without asking anyone: an unknown key says nothing in itself, and what matters in this model is the place it occupies in the graph of relationships.

The graph data already exists in events. The NIP-02 follow list is a kind `3` event that contains one `p` tag per followed account. The NIP-51 mute list (kind `10000`) designates the keys, hashtags, words, or threads that a user no longer wants to see. Let's take an example. Alice follows Bob and Charlie. Charlie follows Dave. A client that wants to judge Dave from Alice's point of view can take into account that he is followed by an account that Alice follows, or that several accounts in Alice's circle have muted him in their public list.

No NIP fixes the algorithm: each client or provider chooses the depth of the graph, the weightings, and the weight of mutes. This choice has a cost. Some of these computations require a large number of events and a lot of computing power, which a client can hardly provide alone. NIP-85, *Trusted Assertions*, addresses this problem: the user declares in a kind `10040` event the providers they trust, and these providers publish signed addressable events, of kind `30382` for an account, `30383` for an event, `30384` for an addressable event, and `30385` for an external identifier. A `rank` tag, a normalized integer from 0 to 100, carries the score there, and other tags carry follower or zap counts. Each provider computes in its own way (a follower count may or may not exclude muted keys), and the user chooses the one whose method suits them. NIP-85 requires separate service keys for each algorithm.

The principle has its limits. Trust remains subjective: two users can get different scores for the same account. It also shifts trust to the score provider, since NIP-85 provides no way to verify its computation. Finally, it does not work everywhere: NIP-17 private messages go through throwaway keys, so relays cannot apply to them an anti-spam based on the reputation of a key or on the web of trust.

NIP-85 was added to the NIP repository on January 22, 2026, at the initiative of Vitor Pamplona, and remains at `draft` status.

*Not to be confused with Nostr proof of work, another way of limiting spam, which relies on a computational cost rather than on the relationships between accounts.*
