Acronym for *Winternitz One-Time Signature*. One-time hash-based signature that shortens Lamport's by signing several bits with a single secret, thanks to a hash chain. Its security relies only on a hash function, but a key must sign only one message (see HASH-BASED SIGNATURE).

In Lamport's scheme (see LAMPORT SIGNATURE), each secret signs only one bit. With WOTS, Alice draws a secret $x$ and hashes it in a chain: $F(x)$, then $F(F(x))$, and so on, where $F$ is a hash function. She sets a parameter $w$ (the Winternitz parameter) and publishes the end of the chain, $y = F^{w-1}(x)$, that is, $x$ hashed $w - 1$ times. To sign a digit $d$ between 0 and $w - 1$, she reveals the link $F^d(x)$. Bob hashes it $w - 1 - d$ more times and verifies that it lands on $y$. With $w = 16$, a single secret signs one hexadecimal digit, that is, 4 bits. A 32-byte (256-bit) hash is thus split into 64 digits, hence into 64 chains for the message (67 with the checksum, see below), whereas Lamport reveals 256 secrets, drawn from a key of 512 secrets.

This idea has a flaw: hashing works in only one direction, toward the end of the chain. Anyone who sees $F^9(x)$ can compute $F^{10}(x)$ and claim that Alice signed a larger digit. To prevent this, Alice adds a checksum: she sums, over all the digits of the message, the distance that separates them from the maximum, $C = \sum (w - 1 - d_i)$, and signs this sum with a few more chains. Increasing a digit of the message lowers $C$, so at least one digit of the checksum would have to move back along its chain, which would require inverting the hash function. RFC 8554 summarizes the role of this sum: any attempt at forgery that manipulates the elements of an existing signature will be detected.

Let's take an example reduced to an 8-bit message, `0x9C`, with $w = 16$. It gives 2 digits, 9 and 12. The checksum is $(15 - 9) + (15 - 12) = 9$, which is written on 2 digits, 0 and 9. Alice therefore uses 4 chains, with 4 secrets $x_1$ to $x_4$, and reveals $F^9(x_1)$, $F^{12}(x_2)$, $x_3$, and $F^9(x_4)$. Bob hashes these values 6, 3, 15, and 6 times and compares the chain ends with the public key. A forger wanting to sign `0x9D` (digits 9 and 13) can advance the second chain by one step, but the checksum would drop to 8, that is, the digits 0 and 8: the forger would need $F^8(x_4)$, while knowing only $F^9(x_4)$. This example illustrates the principle and ignores the byte alignment of the RFC 8391 pseudocode.

Sizes depend on $w$. With `SHA256` ($n = 32$ bytes) and $w = 16$, WOTS+ has 67 chains: 64 for the message and 3 for the checksum. The signature, like the uncompressed public key, is therefore 67 × 32 = 2,144 bytes, against 8,192 bytes for a Lamport signature. Generating the key costs 67 × 15 = 1,005 chain steps, that is, as many calls to the function F (RFC 8391 adds 2 PRF calls per step), and signing and verifying each require about half of that on average. A larger $w$ shortens the signature but lengthens the chains, and therefore the computation. RFC 8554 shows this for LM-OTS (SHA-256, $n = 32$): the signature goes from 8,516 bytes with 1 bit per chain to 1,124 bytes with 8 bits per chain. RFC 8391 is limited to $w = 4$ or $w = 16$ and defines parameter sets only for $w = 16$.

One-time use remains the central constraint. If a key signs 2 different messages, RFC 8391 warns that the scheme is no longer secure, and NIST (SP 800-208) writes that an attacker could then produce forgeries. The authors of the paper *Oops, I Did It Again* (Leon Groot Bruinderink and Andreas Hülsing) studied this case: for WOTS, security degrades much faster than for Lamport as soon as 2 messages are signed, and the usual parameters no longer offer any reasonable level. On Bitcoin, BIP-0360 notes that common practices such as RBF and CPFP would risk revealing private keys if no stateless signature is used.

Several variants exist. WOTS+, proposed by Hülsing at AFRICACRYPT 2013, adds to each step of the chain a public random XOR mask and a keyed hash function (RFC 8391 derives them from a pseudorandom function with a public seed). Its security proof relies on second-preimage resistance, one-wayness, and the pseudorandomness of the function, without requiring collision resistance, which allows shorter hashes at equal security, whereas the original WOTS also relies on a form of collision resistance. FIPS 205 uses a version of WOTS+ whose chaining has no mask and no per-step key (each hash receives only the public seed and an address), which Mikhail Kudinov and Jonas Nick call WOTS-TW. WOTS+C, from SPHINCS+C (Kudinov, Hülsing, Ronen, and Yogev, 2023), removes the checksum chains: the signer keeps rehashing the message with a counter until the sum of the digits reaches a fixed constant, which the verifier checks. Increasing one digit then forces another to decrease, which would require inverting the hash function. SHRINCS derives from it signatures of 32 chains of 16 bytes and a sum fixed at 240.

WOTS is a building block, rarely a standalone scheme. XMSS arranges many of them under the root of a Merkle tree, SLH-DSA stacks them in a hypertree (never signing the user's message directly: it signs only FORS public keys or tree roots, and FIPS 205 advises modules against exposing WOTS+ alone), and SHRINCS uses WOTS+C on its stateful path.

On Bitcoin, no WOTS signature can validate a spend on mainnet. On June 8, 2025, Conduition published on the bitcoindev mailing list a prototype of verification in Script with `OP_CAT` (see BIP-0347), tested on a Bitcoin Inquisition test node. It has WOTS sign the `SHA256` of a BIP340 signature verified by `OP_CHECKSIG`, split into 64 words of 4 bits, with $w = 16$. Instead of checksum chains, the script adds up the 64 words and requires a fixed sum of 480 (Conduition corrected an initial value of 512 on July 8, 2025), and the signer redoes their signature with a new nonce until it lands on that sum. It does not implement WOTS+ for lack of `OP_XOR` in Script. The witness weighs 7,948 bytes, or 2,070 vB for the spending transaction, then about 7,212 bytes, or about 1,886 vB for the transaction (1,803 vB for the witness alone), after an optimization proposed by Anthony Towns and refined by Conduition, against about 10,700 bytes (script and witness combined, according to Conduition's estimate) for Jeremy Rubin's Lamport scheme (2021). Conduition sees it as an escape hatch for a quantum emergency rather than a default signature. It requires disabling Taproot's key path, otherwise a quantum attacker would bypass the script. Jonas Nick had already implemented W-OTS+ with the opcodes of the Great Script Restoration, in his GreatRSI interpreter (repository created on November 7, 2024), for a witness of about 24 kB.

In the text that Ralph Merkle dates to 1979, *A Certified Digital Signature*, submitted to Ron Rivest, never published by the journal, and finally published at CRYPTO '89, he credits Robert Winternitz, of Stanford's mathematics department, with an improvement suggested shortly before publication, which further reduces the size of the signed message by a factor of 4 to 8 by trading space for computation. Merkle immediately points out the flaw of the digit that can be advanced and fixes it with an extension of his method of counting 0 bits. The sources consulted cite no publication by Winternitz himself.

*The scheme is also called W-OTS, and Hülsing's version W-OTS+ or WOTS+. RFC 8554 calls it the Lamport-Diffie-Winternitz-Merkle system. FIPS 205 names WOTS+ a variant whose chaining differs from that of RFC 8391.*
