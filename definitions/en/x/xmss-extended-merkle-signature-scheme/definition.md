Acronym for *eXtended Merkle Signature Scheme*. Stateful hash-based signature that gathers many WOTS+ one-time keys under the root of a Merkle tree, so that a single public key can sign a number of messages fixed in advance. The signer must remember which one-time keys have already been used (see HASH-BASED SIGNATURE).

Let's take the XMSS-SHA2_10_256 parameter set of RFC 8391. Alice derives $2^{10}$ = 1,024 WOTS+ keys (see WOTS - WINTERNITZ ONE-TIME SIGNATURE), each made of 67 values of 32 bytes. She compresses the public key of each one into a 32-byte leaf, then hashes the leaves two by two, level by level, up to the root of a binary tree of height 10. Her public key contains only the root, a 32-byte public seed `SEED`, and a 4-byte algorithm identifier, for 68 bytes in total. Her private key additionally contains the secrets (the seed that generates the WOTS+ keys, an `SK_PRF` key) and an index, initially 0, which designates the next unused leaf.

To sign, Alice computes a randomized digest of the message, signs it with the WOTS+ key of the leaf designated by the index, then attaches the 10 sibling nodes along the path from that leaf to the root (the authentication path). She then increments the index, and only after that publishes the signature: the RFC forbids releasing the signature before the private key has been updated. The signature consists of 4 bytes of index, 32 bytes of randomness, 67 × 32 bytes of WOTS+ signature, and 10 × 32 bytes of path, for 2,500 bytes. Bob reconstructs the WOTS+ public key from the signature, compresses it into a leaf, climbs the tree with the 10 nodes provided and the index, and accepts if the resulting root equals the one in the public key.

The height $h$ of the tree sets the number of signatures, $2^h$. The mandatory parameter sets of RFC 8391 are XMSS-SHA2_10_256, XMSS-SHA2_16_256, and XMSS-SHA2_20_256, whose signatures weigh 2,500, 2,692, and 2,820 bytes. In exchange, key generation goes from about 1.2 million hash calls to about 1.27 billion, according to the table in RFC 8391 (WOTS+ chains, leaves, and tree included). To go further, the RFC defines XMSS^MT, a multi-tree variant: XMSS trees stacked in $d$ layers, where the trees of the bottom layer sign the message and each tree of the upper layers signs the root of a tree of the layer below. Its recommended set, XMSSMT-SHA2_60/3_256, allows $2^{60}$ messages with signatures of 8,392 bytes.

State is the practical difficulty. If the same leaf signs 2 messages, RFC 8391 warns that no security guarantee remains and that a forgery becomes feasible; its authors advise against using the scheme anywhere other than in systems that prevent state reuse. NIST approves XMSS and XMSS^MT, like LMS and HSS (RFC 8554, April 2019), in its SP 800-208 recommendation of October 29, 2020, but with strict conditions: keys generated and used in FIPS 140 validated hardware modules of at least level 3, no export of the private key, even encrypted, and an update of the index in non-volatile memory before the signature is exported. Copying the key creates a risk of one-time key reuse: NIST instead proposes distributing a 2-level XMSS^MT across several modules, and judges these schemes unsuited to general use. They target long-lived applications that would be impractical to migrate later; NIST cites the authentication of firmware updates for constrained devices as a possible example.

In terms of security, XMSS relies only on properties of the hash function, and RFC 8391 says it stays secure even if the collision resistance of that function is broken. Against a quantum computer, Grover's algorithm forces the size of hashes to be doubled: the `SHA256` set keeps 128 bits of security according to the RFC.

For a Bitcoin wallet, state raises a backup problem. In their December 2025 paper, Mikhail Kudinov and Jonas Nick (Blockstream Research) point out that restoring an outdated backup could cause state reuse and allow forgeries, and they therefore focus on stateless schemes (see SLH-DSA). On Delving Bitcoin, Conduition also raises the case of a seed duplicated across several devices and that of a system cloned from a disk backup, and compares it to restoring an outdated state of a Lightning channel. On February 9, 2026, Ethan Heilman judged it reasonable to consider stateful hash-based signatures such as XMSS, Winternitz, or Lamport as a fallback signature, while preferring SLH-DSA. The Chaincode report (Anthony Milton and Clara Shikhelman, May 2025) cites the cryptocurrency Quantum Resistant Ledger among those that use XMSS in place of ECDSA.

As of October 25, 2026, no Bitcoin consensus rule incorporates XMSS and no BIP in the official repository specifies it. BIP-0360 (P2MR) cited it: its version 0.2.0, of September 28, 2024, added Winternitz and XMSS to its table of post-quantum algorithms, then version 0.3.0, of October 21, 2024, replaced XMSS with CRYSTALS-Dilithium, because of NIST approval and size constraints. The SHRINCS project takes up the idea in the form of a "flexible" XMSS tree of WOTS+C keys, whose shape the signer chooses (unbalanced, the first signatures being the shortest), combined with a stateless fallback for the case where the state is lost.

XMSS was proposed in 2011 by Johannes Buchmann, Erik Dahmen, and Andreas Hülsing (*XMSS - A Practical Forward Secure Signature Scheme Based on Minimal Security Assumptions*, PQCrypto 2011, Taipei), with a security proof in the standard model and better efficiency than the original scheme (the authors report signatures less than a quarter the size of those of MSS-SPR, the most efficient minimal-assumption scheme known at the time). The original scheme is the work of Ralph Merkle, who describes in his June 1979 Stanford thesis the method that arranges one-time keys under the root of an authentication tree. RFC 8391, published in May 2018 by Hülsing, Denis Butin, Stefan-Lukas Gazdag, Joost Rijneveld, and Aziz Mohaisen, is an informational document of the IRTF Crypto Forum Research Group (CFRG), not an IETF standard.

*The Chaincode report wrongly describes it as a one-time scheme: XMSS signs several messages, and it is each WOTS+ key that signs only one.*
