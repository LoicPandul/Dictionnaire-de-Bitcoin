Set of techniques that protect the information of a quantum computer against errors in its physical qubits, by spreading it across several of them to form a logical qubit. A quantum error-correcting code, such as the surface code, lowers the error rate of the logical qubit exponentially as physical qubits are added to it, but only if the physical error rate stays below a threshold specific to the code. Google crossed this threshold in 2024 with its Willow processor. Without error correction, no quantum computer could carry through to the end the tens of millions of logical operations that Shor's algorithm requires against a Bitcoin key: at most 90 million Toffoli gates with 1,200 logical qubits, or at most 70 million with 1,450, according to the estimate published in 2026 by Ryan Babbush and his coauthors (Google Quantum AI), which trades time for space.

The classical recipe does not carry over as is. To protect a bit, it can be written 3 times and, if one of the copies changes by mistake, the majority value is kept. With a qubit, this check runs into a property of quantum physics: a quantum system cannot be observed without disturbing it, so reading the qubits to compare their values would destroy the superposition they carry. As the physicist John Preskill explains, error correction gets around the obstacle by encoding the information in a highly entangled state spread over many qubits. The environment, which acts on only a few qubits at a time, can then neither read this information nor damage it. What remains is to spot errors without ever reading the information itself, by measuring only relations between qubits.

The surface code organizes this check on a grid. Data qubits, which together carry the information of the logical qubit, alternate with measurement qubits. At each cycle, each measurement qubit queries its neighbors (4 data qubits in the middle of the grid) and extracts a parity from them, that is, the information “have these qubits kept the same agreement among themselves as in the previous cycle?”, without learning anything about their value. When a data qubit suffers an error, at least two of the measurement qubits around it change their result from one cycle to the next. A classical computer, the decoder, receives these results cycle after cycle, deduces the most likely error, and takes it into account in the final readout. On Google's 72-qubit processor, a cycle lasts 1.1 microseconds, and the decoder returns its correction 63 microseconds on average after receiving the last cycle of measurements (distance-5 code): its throughput must keep up with that of the results, one cycle every 1.1 microseconds, and the experiment makes it hold up to one million cycles.

The size of the grid sets the protection. In Google's layout, a surface code of distance $d$ has $d \times d$ data qubits, and the larger $d$ is, the more simultaneous errors are needed to corrupt the logical qubit. If $p$ is the error rate of the physical qubits and $p_{thr}$ the threshold of the code, the logical error $\varepsilon_d$ approximately follows:

$$\varepsilon_d \propto \left(\frac{p}{p_{thr}}\right)^{(d+1)/2}$$

As long as $p$ stays below $p_{thr}$, each time the distance increases by 2, the logical error is divided by a factor $\Lambda \approx p_{thr}/p$, the more exactly the further $p$ is below the threshold. Above the threshold, adding qubits instead makes the error rise, because beyond the threshold, errors pile up in a larger grid faster than the code can correct them. According to Austin Fowler and his coauthors (2012), the surface code tolerates error rates on the order of 1% per operation, against about 0.002% for other codes, such as those of Steane and Bacon-Shor, laid out on a grid, and it requires only interactions between neighboring qubits. Its authors see it as one of the most realistic approaches for building a solid-state quantum computer.

The Willow processor showed this behavior in practice. In their August 2024 paper, published in Nature on December 9, 2024, Google's teams compare surface codes of distance 3, 5, and 7, that is, grids of 3 by 3, 5 by 5, and 7 by 7 data qubits. At each step of 2 in distance (from 3 to 5, then from 5 to 7), the logical error is divided by $\Lambda = 2.14$, and the distance-7 logical qubit keeps its information longer than the best of its physical qubits (the details of this logical qubit are given in the logical qubit entry). It is the first time a processor has operated clearly below the threshold, which Google presents as a challenge pursued for nearly 30 years.

This result remains very far from a cryptographic computation. To break a 2,048-bit RSA key, Craig Gidney aims in 2025 for a logical error of $10^{-15}$ per qubit and per cycle, about $10^{12}$ times lower than the 0.143% per cycle of Willow's logical qubit. Google's experiments on repetition codes, up to distance 29, also run into rare correlated errors, which hit several qubits at once about once an hour and whose origin was not understood in 2024, with Google proposing since 2025 to see them as the effect of ionizing radiation impacts on the chip: they set an apparent floor of about $10^{-10}$ per cycle, which enlarging the code is not enough to cross. Finally, correction is paid for in physical qubits: from 10 to over 10,000 per logical qubit depending on the code, the physical error rate, and the date of the estimate (101 for Willow's logical qubit, on the order of 400 to 1,400 in the 2025 and 2026 estimates for breaking RSA-2048 or secp256k1, according to the estimates detailed in the logical qubit entry), and part of the recent progress concerns this cost, with yoked surface codes, which store idle logical qubits more densely.

The idea dates from the 1990s. Peter Shor published in 1995 the first quantum error correction scheme, a year after his factoring algorithm. In November 1996, Dorit Aharonov and Michael Ben-Or showed, independently of Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek (October 1996), that a quantum computation of any size can remain reliable provided the physical error rate is below a constant: this is the threshold theorem, which then gave a threshold of about $10^{-6}$ for their codes. In 1997, Alexei Kitaev described inherently fault-tolerant computation, based on a 2-dimensional system. His toric codes, in which the qubits are spread over the surface of a torus, then gave rise to surface codes, planar versions developed by Bravyi and Kitaev as well as by Freedman and Meyer.

*Quantum error correction is often abbreviated QEC. A fault-tolerant quantum computer is one that carries its computation through to the end despite imperfect operations, by operating on logical qubits protected by this correction.*
