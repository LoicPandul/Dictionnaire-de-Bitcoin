Unconventional monetary policy in which a central bank massively buys financial assets, mainly government bonds and asset-backed securities, to inject liquidity into the financial system. The objective is to lower long-term interest rates, stimulate credit, and ultimately support economic activity when conventional tools (such as cutting the policy rate) have reached their limits, notably when that rate is close to zero (zero lower bound).

QE was widely deployed by the major central banks starting with the 2008 financial crisis. The US Federal Reserve (Fed), the European Central Bank (ECB), and the Bank of Japan (BoJ) ran successive programs that considerably inflated the size of their balance sheets. This monetary expansion is criticized for its redistributive effects: by pushing up the price of financial assets (stocks, bonds, real estate), QE benefits the holders of these assets more than the real economy, which illustrates the Cantillon effect.

In the Bitcoin community, quantitative easing is often cited as one of the fundamental motivations for adopting BTC. Faced with a money creation perceived as unlimited and discretionary, Bitcoin offers a transparent and immutable monetary policy, with issuance capped at 21 million units.
