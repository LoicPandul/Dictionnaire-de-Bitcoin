Day from which a quantum computer can break the public-key cryptography currently in use. For Bitcoin, it is the moment a CRQC (Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer) appears, a quantum computer able to run Shor's algorithm against the secp256k1 curve, and thus to recover a private key from the corresponding public key. ECDSA and Schnorr signatures, which both rely on the hardness of the discrete logarithm on this curve, then no longer protect funds whose public key is known. Nobody knows whether this day will come, or when.

Before Q-day, a public key visible on the blockchain cannot yet be exploited: according to the Chaincode Labs report published in May 2025, deriving the private key from it would take today's supercomputers trillions of years. A CRQC could cut this computation down to a few hours or days. Let's take an example. Alice has held a 50 BTC P2PK output since 2010, whose script contains her public key in the clear. After Q-day, an attacker equipped with a CRQC reads this key on the blockchain, derives the private key, then signs a transaction that sends the 50 BTC to an address the attacker controls. For nodes, this signature is valid, and nothing distinguishes it from a signature by Alice.

Q-day does not threaten all outputs at the same time. BIP-0360 distinguishes two situations. A long-exposure attack targets a public key that is already known: that of a P2PK, P2MS, or P2TR output, which the script shows on-chain, that of a reused address, or one that an xpub let circulate. The attacker has all the time needed as long as the funds do not move. A short-exposure attack targets a key revealed at spending time, while the transaction waits for confirmation in the mempool: the attacker must break the key before the transaction is mined. BIP-0360 considers it likely that long-exposure attacks become possible first, since the others require a faster quantum computer. For Jameson Lopp, one of the worst scenarios would be that breaking a key takes less than 10 minutes, which would also expose pending transactions. According to BIP-0361, as of March 1, 2026, more than 34% of bitcoin sit on outputs whose public key has already been revealed on-chain.

The date of Q-day is unknown, and estimates diverge. In February 2026, the Netherlands Court of Audit reports that the Dutch intelligence service (AIVD) considers it possible as early as 2030, for current cryptography in general. In the survey published in December 2024 by the Global Risk Institute, 10 of the 32 experts polled estimated at about 50% or more the probability that, within 10 years, a quantum computer exists that can factor a 2,048-bit number in less than 24 hours, which is enough to break an RSA key of that size; the question was therefore about RSA, not about Bitcoin's curve. BIP-0360, for its part, takes no position on any timeline.

Q-day may also go unnoticed. BIP-0361 points out that an attacker could compute private keys and then wait weeks or months before moving the funds, discreetly, so as not to alert those watching the chain: the actual day would then be known only much later. Jameson Lopp, first author of BIP-0361, adds that breaking keys can be done privately, without broadcasting anything: an attacker who succeeds quickly would have an interest in breaking as many as possible before revealing themselves, and if each key takes days or weeks, would start with the richest ones that have been inactive the longest.

The pace at which Q-day would approach is itself debated. Optech considers a gradual rise in quantum computer capability likely; as long as it remains publicly observable, Bitcoin could keep its current cryptography, then switch to post-quantum cryptography when that switch appears about to become necessary. In July 2026, on Delving Bitcoin, Pieter Wuille notes a frequent argument to the contrary: one should not count on a regular series of milestones being passed before Q-day. He concludes that one should not wait for such milestones to deploy post-quantum output types, while believing that milestones will come, but at a compressed pace, shortly before a CRQC becomes feasible.

Consensus cannot know the date of Q-day, but it could react to a proof. In June 2026, Pieter Wuille describes on the bitcoindev list a tripwire, an idea he attributes to Tadge Dryja. The soft fork that would create a new post-quantum output type would also fix a NUMS (Nothing Up My Sleeve) point, that is, a point on the curve whose private key nobody knows. If a mined transaction succeeded in an `OP_CHECKSIG` with this point, it would prove that the discrete logarithm on secp256k1 is broken, and the elliptic curve opcodes and spending paths of that output type would be disabled from the next block. Wuille sees it as an upper bound, the limit beyond which the elliptic curve must no longer be used, and not as a forecast; he later considered other forms of proof and a delay of a few days before the disabling. He adds the miner lockdown, suggested by Sjors Provoost, which would let a majority of the hash power trigger the same disabling if it judges Q-day too close.

Noticing Q-day does not pose the same problem for all users. In September 2026, Conduition remarks on Delving Bitcoin that consensus mechanisms like the tripwire must continuously answer the question “Is it Q-day today?”. For him, the holder of dormant coins on a P2MR output whose public key has never been revealed only needs to know, before their next transaction, whether Q-day has occurred since their last connection. Hot wallets, such as those of Lightning nodes or exchanges, must on the contrary detect it very quickly.

Post-quantum migration proposals are organized around this date. Before Q-day, the goal is to move as many bitcoin as possible to post-quantum outputs. Pieter Wuille thus proposes in July 2026 to deploy two output types: P2TRv2, a variant of P2TR equipped with a post-quantum signature opcode, the tripwire, and the miner lockdown, and which keeps the cost of a P2TR spend, to ease migration, and a P2MR-based type for the long term. After Q-day, rescue protocols such as DropKick, sketched by Conduition on the bitcoindev list in August 2026, would let latecomers recover their coins by proving knowledge that the attacker does not have, provided they were activated before Q-day; BIP-0361 considers it impossible, given current knowledge, to build one for P2PK outputs. BIP-0361 also approaches the problem from the other side, replacing the uncertainty of Q-day with an announced schedule. Its phase A would forbid sending funds to vulnerable outputs 160,000 blocks (about 3 years) after its activation, and its phase B would, 2 years later, subject ECDSA and Schnorr spends to a rescue protocol.

The term is not specific to Bitcoin. In its February 2026 press release, the Netherlands Court of Audit defines Q-Day as the day when malicious actors can use quantum computers to break current cryptography. Among Bitcoin developers, it appears in BIP-0361 and in the 2026 discussions on Delving Bitcoin and the bitcoindev list, where Pieter Wuille, Anthony Towns, and Conduition set what must be ready “before Q-day” against what will only be useful “after Q-day.”

*Also written Q-Day, with a capital D. Q-day refers to a date, not to be confused with the CRQC, the machine that would make it possible.*
