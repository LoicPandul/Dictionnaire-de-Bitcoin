Consensus rule that, once ordinary ECDSA and Schnorr spends are restricted, would let the true owner of a vulnerable output spend it by proving knowledge that a quantum attacker does not have. The term appears in BIP-0361, which plans to subject classical spends to a “quantum-safe rescue protocol” in its phase B, without describing any of them precisely. It therefore designates a family of mechanisms yet to be designed, more than a single protocol.

The starting condition is what the BIP calls a knowledge asymmetry: a context in which the legitimate holder knows something the attacker does not. Any such context can, in theory, become a rescue protocol. conduition defines it, in a message of July 14, 2026, as the witness to a relation that is hard even for a quantum computer and committed on-chain before Q-day, or, in plain terms, a piece of high-entropy information that the honest holder knows, that a future cryptographically relevant quantum computer would not know, and that it could neither compute nor easily guess. On this principle, nodes would require proof of this knowledge in addition to the signature, which the BIP expresses with the verb “to encumber”: making the spend heavier instead of forbidding it.

Let's take an example. Alice uses a BIP-0032 wallet whose P2TR addresses derive from a branch that includes at least one hardened derivation. A quantum computer could recover the private key of each of her outputs from the visible public key, but not the parent extended private key, located above the hardened derivation, which it would be very unlikely to know. When phase B applied, Alice would sign her spend as before and attach a proof that she knows this parent key. Nodes verify both; the attacker, who can only forge the signature, sees the spend rejected, provided the proof is bound to the transaction. The BIP cites this example and two ways to provide the proof: protocols based on zk-STARKs, for which ongoing research suggests that proofs could scale efficiently, and the commit-reveal scheme, which it considers likely to be more efficient at the cost of a more delicate multi-step security model.

Not all outputs lend themselves to a rescue. Hashed addresses (P2PKH, P2SH, P2WPKH, P2WSH) whose preimage has never been revealed, on-chain or off-chain, have one: the output alone shows that a piece of data, a public key or a script, is hidden behind the hash. Most P2TR outputs have one in their internal key: the output key is the internal key tweaked by a hash, as BIP-0086 provides for single-key outputs, and this internal key remains unknown to the attacker as long as it has not been published, which excludes outputs with a NUMS internal key or outputs already spent through a script path. BIP-0032 wallets with hardened derivation have one in the parent key. Conversely, P2PK outputs generally have none known: BIP-0361 writes that building one for them is not believed possible today, and its authors support an Hourglass-type rule for this case. A hashed address whose public key has already been exposed, outside a BIP-0032 derivation, has none either. Part of the supply is therefore out of reach of a full coverage, though nobody knows which part: BIP-0361 only indicates that as of March 1, 2026, more than 34% of bitcoin already have a public key visible on-chain, some of which retain an asymmetry (P2TR outputs, hardened BIP-0032 derivation). If one or more protocols covered most of the supply, restricting ECDSA and Schnorr would be, according to the BIP, only slightly confiscatory at most.

Several proofs have been proposed. On April 8, 2026, Olaoluwa Osuntokun published a proof of concept on bitcoindev: a zk-STARK proof that a Taproot output key derives from a seed along a BIP-0086 path. His first measurements give a proof of about 1.7 MB, produced in about 55 seconds and verified in under 2 seconds, and, the next day, a variant that proves only the knowledge of a parent extended private key above a hardened derivation brings it down to about 235 kB. Optech (newsletter #403, May 1, 2026) notes that he produced a working prover and verifier. On July 14, 2026, conduition proposed the Taproot internal key as the asymmetry for P2TR key-path spends, and Shinobi, on Delving Bitcoin and bitcoindev, combined three mechanisms (hierarchical BIP-0032 proofs, proofs timestamped before the deadline, commit-reveal) that would cover, in his view, all holders of hashed addresses who control their keys, as long as the public keys remain secret. conduition observes that almost any asymmetry authenticated by a zero-knowledge proof can also be authenticated by commit-reveal, and vice versa.

Most of these proposals meet objections. Abubakar Sadiq Ismail points out that Osuntokun's proof does not commit to the transaction: any observer can copy it, which Osuntokun had himself noted as future work. On April 13, 2026, Alex judges that a 1.7 MB proof has no realistic use and worries about the attack surface of a virtual machine of several million lines. conduition notes that leaked account xpubs allow the proof for a hashed address to be forged, and that any list of keys already exposed would remain incomplete, for lack of any trace of off-chain exposures.

BIP-0361, by Jameson Lopp, Christian Papathanasiou, Ian Smith, Joe Ross, Steve Vaile, and Pierre-Luc Dallaire-Demers, received its number on February 11, 2026 with Draft status and was merged into the BIP repository on April 14, 2026 (PR #1895). Its phase A forbids sending funds to vulnerable outputs, about 3 years after activation; its phase B, 2 years later, restricts classical spends. In the April 14, 2026 version, phase B made these spends invalid, with a phase C to be defined to recover funds through a BIP-0039 seed proof. On April 17, conduition amended it (PR #2146, merged April 20): the term “quantum-safe rescue protocol” enters the text, phase C disappears, and phase B restricts spending by encumbering it with a rescue protocol. During the review, he judges the technical part “barren”, for lack of defined terms and precise conditions, criticizes the absence of any concrete rescue, and notes that adding a rescue later would require a hard fork. The BIP points to no implementation, and publishing a BIP, as the repository README points out, means neither community agreement nor imminent adoption. See post-quantum migration for the full plan, QRAMP for another proposed deadline, and Satoshi's coins for P2PK outputs, for which no rescue protocol is known.
