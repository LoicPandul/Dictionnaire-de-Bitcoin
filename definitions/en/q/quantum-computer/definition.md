Machine that computes on qubits rather than bits, relying on three phenomena of quantum mechanics (superposition, entanglement, and interference) to solve certain problems much faster than a classical computer. This gain applies only to specific classes of problems. Two of them affect Bitcoin: the discrete logarithm, on which ECDSA and Schnorr signatures depend, and unstructured search, to which finding a preimage of a hash function and mining both reduce. As of 2026, no known quantum computer is capable of breaking Bitcoin's cryptography: such a machine has a name of its own, the CRQC.

A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit can be 0, 1, or in a superposition of both. Several qubits can also be entangled, meaning that they share a single quantum state instead of each having its own. Consider 2 qubits: where 2 bits hold only one of the combinations `00`, `01`, `10`, or `11`, 2 qubits can hold a superposition of all 4 at once. With 3 qubits, there are 8 combinations, and each added qubit doubles that number. The operations of a quantum computer act on all these combinations at the same time.

This does not mean that a quantum computer tries every solution to a problem in one go. At the end of the computation, the qubits must be measured, and the measurement yields only a single, randomly drawn result: each combination carries an amplitude, whose squared modulus gives the probability of obtaining it. As physicist Stephen Jordan explains in a NIST article, measurement extracts only a small amount of information about all the computations carried out in superposition. A quantum algorithm therefore works by making the amplitudes interfere, like waves that reinforce or cancel each other, so that the amplitude of the right answer grows and those of the wrong answers shrink before the measurement. Grover's algorithm gives a concrete picture: it starts from a superposition in which each of the $N$ possible answers has the same amplitude, flips the sign of the amplitude of the right answer, then reflects all the amplitudes about their average, which makes the amplitude of the right answer grow. After about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}$ repetitions, the measurement lands on it almost certainly. For $N$ equal to 1 million, about 785 repetitions are needed, whereas a classical search takes 500,000 attempts on average.

The problems sped up in this way are few. Shor's algorithm, published in 1994, factors large integers and computes discrete logarithms in a number of steps polynomial in the size of the data. Run on a large enough machine, it would break RSA as well as ECDSA and Schnorr signatures, whose security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm on the secp256k1 curve. Grover's algorithm, for its part, provides only a quadratic speedup for brute-force search: according to Bitcoin Optech, it reduces the preimage resistance of `SHA256` from 256 to 128 bits, a loss that can be offset by moving to a longer function such as `SHA512`. NIST mainly cites another use, the one Feynman envisioned: the simulation of molecules and materials, which classical computers can approach only with great effort.

Building such a computer remains difficult, because qubits are fragile. According to NIST, a stray electric or magnetic field, a temperature change, or even a cosmic ray is enough to destroy a superposition or an entanglement, and the qubit then behaves like an ordinary bit. The best machines have hundreds of qubits and make about one error every 1,000 operations, compared with about one in $10^{18}$ computations on a classical computer. Physical qubits come in several forms: trapped ions, which keep their superposition for a long time but compute slowly; superconducting circuits cooled near absolute zero, which are fast and manufactured like chips but more fragile; neutral atoms; or photons. So-called topological qubits, which would withstand disturbances better, are under study, with no definitive proof that any has been built. To compute for a long time despite these errors, quantum error correction is needed, which groups many physical qubits into one reliable logical qubit.

Current machines therefore remain small and noisy. Physicist John Preskill coined the acronym NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) in December 2017, in a talk published in 2018, to designate computers of 50 to a few hundred qubits, whose noise limits the size of the computations. Google's Willow processor, announced on December 9, 2024, has 105 physical qubits. It is the first processor to have convincingly shown that adding qubits to the error correction makes the error go down instead of up. It also performed, in under 5 minutes, a random circuit sampling (RCS) computation that, according to Google, would take $10^{25}$ years on one of the fastest supercomputers; but according to Google, this test had no known practical application, and the Chaincode Labs report (May 2025) notes that no quantum computer has yet beaten a supercomputer on a commercially useful task. The same report cites IBM's Heron processors (133 physical qubits, December 2023), Quantinuum's 56-qubit trapped-ion machine (June 2024), and Microsoft's Majorana 1 chip (February 2025), whose claimed topological qubits were met with skepticism.

These machines are still far from threatening Bitcoin. According to Chaincode, a commercially useful quantum computer would require at least on the order of 1,000 logical qubits, each made of 10 to 1,000 physical qubits depending on the technology. In May 2025, Craig Gidney estimated that a little under one million physical qubits, with an error rate of 0.1%, would be needed to factor a 2,048-bit RSA key in under a week. Estimates specific to Bitcoin's curve are detailed in the logical qubit and CRQC (cryptographically relevant quantum computer) entries. Bitcoin Optech considers it likely that the capacity and reliability of quantum computers will progress gradually; as long as this progress remains publicly observable, Bitcoin could keep using its current cryptography, then move to post-quantum cryptography when that move becomes necessary. Pieter Wuille, in July 2026, instead expects the milestones to arrive at a compressed pace, shortly before a CRQC becomes feasible, and refuses to wait for them before deploying post-quantum output types.

The idea goes back to 1981: at a meeting of physicists near Boston, Richard Feynman proposes “simulating physics with computers”, quantum ones, a talk often presented as the starting point of the field. In 1994, Shor's algorithm makes the quantum computer a national security question, since it threatens public-key encryption. Peter Shor then introduces, in 1995, quantum error correction, the technique on which plans for reliable machines rest, and Lov Grover publishes his search algorithm in 1996.

*Not to be confused with quantum annealing machines, another technology that targets certain physics and optimization problems, nor with quantum cryptography, which uses the properties of quantum physics to exchange encryption keys.*
